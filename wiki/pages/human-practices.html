Overview

As we built AETH3R, our team held fast to the following principle: scientific progress in synthetic biology only achieves real purpose when it is aligned with the human realities it intends to serve.

We began our project ideation by exploring how synthetic biology could address unmet needs in oncology, a journey that quickly brought us to Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC). Refusing to rely solely on literature reviews and driven by a sense of responsibility, we viewed Human Practices as our foundational compass, embedding stakeholder feedback into our design loop from day one. Engaging oncologists and translational researchers brought us to the harsh clinical reality of NSCLC. Shadowing workflows at the European Interbalkan Medical Center and sitting down with a lung cancer patient to hear his story reoriented our work. He was no longer a theoretical case study. His lived experience gave urgency and ethical weight to every decision we made.

Instilling these clinical imperatives in our mission, we initiated a continuous feedback loop with stakeholders. Early dialogues with clinicians validated the novelty of Targeted Protein Degradation over traditional small-molecule inhibition, showing us that removing oncogenic drivers offers vast therapeutic potential. Stakeholders also confirmed the power of a dual-targeting strategy against KRAS and MDM2 in NSCLC.

When evaluating how to design these protein degraders, consultations with small-molecule PROTAC and bioPROTAC specialists always returned to the same critical question: while bioPROTACs offer distinct advantages, how do we overcome their significant hurdles? Our ongoing feedback loop led us to a true lightbulb moment: adopting peptide PROTACs as a hybrid approach that combines the core benefits of both modalities while effectively bypassing their main limitations. While navigating the final challenge of targeted delivery, feedback from nanomedicine specialists helped us compare alternative carriers and ultimately adopt Lipid Nanoparticles (LNPs) as our primary delivery system.

Expert consultations guided every facet of AETH3R. Computational and clinical specialists refined our modeling pipelines and clinical development roadmaps. Feedback from a plethora of stakeholders embedded biosecurity, ethics and sustainable practices directly into our project. It also shaped our inclusive and educational outreach as well as helped us establish a responsible business model and align AETH3R with sustainable standards.

Human Practices gave AETH3R its direction and real societal purpose, connecting stakeholder insights to every step of our project.

Identifying & Contextualizing the Problem

We began by establishing what the clinical problem actually is, rather than assuming it. Oncologists, pulmonologists, a clinical trials lead and a lung cancer patient each reframed the burden of non-small cell lung cancer for us, and together they set the constraints every later design decision had to respect.

Dr. Nikolaos Touroutoglou

Unlocking NSCLC Clinical Realities, Treatment Challenges and Resistance Mechanisms

Stakeholder Profile

Dr. Touroutoglou is an Oncologist based at the Interbalkan Medical Center in Thessaloniki, Greece, since 2004. He has been practicing medicine as an Oncologist and Hematologist at the Nevada Cancer Center, USA, since 1996, and is additionally an Assistant Professor at the School of Medicine, University of Nevada.

Discussion Summary

We met with Dr. Touroutoglou, an oncologist, to better understand the clinical landscape of cancer treatment and to evaluate how our proposed therapeutic approach aligns with current medical needs and challenges. The discussion provided valuable insight into disease epidemiology, mechanisms of resistance, and the practical limitations of existing therapies, helping us ground our project in real clinical reality. Additionally, we gathered crucial feedback on possible targeted degradation targets that arose via literature review, specifically KRAS, EGFR, MDM2, and BCL-XL.

Insights & Recommendations

From a clinical perspective, Dr. Touroutoglou pointed out that there remains a strong unmet need for improved therapies in lung cancer, particularly for patients with adenocarcinoma and high PD-L1 expression. While immunotherapy has improved outcomes, a significant proportion of patients still relapse, and additional biopsies often reveal new mutations such as KRAS or cMET. Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related death, with contributing factors including smoking, which accounts for approximately 30% of cases, as well as environmental pollution, even in non-smokers.

Dr. Touroutoglou initially highlighted an important trend observed in recent years, namely the increasing incidence of cancer in younger populations. He emphasized that, despite significant advances in targeted therapies, many currently available drugs -such as pan-KRAS inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)- often fail to provide long-term solutions due to the rapid development of resistance. While emerging technologies like PROTACs are already being explored, especially in breast cancer, their clinical benefit has not yet significantly surpassed that of existing treatments.

Regarding PROTACs, he noted that while they offer a novel mechanism of action by degrading target proteins, their effects may be temporary, and resistance can still develop. He emphasized that evaluating resistance mechanisms early in the development process is essential, as a therapy that is effective in only a small percentage of patients would not be sufficient in a clinical setting. He also highlighted the importance of multiple targeting strategies, explaining that most successful cancer therapies have evolved from single-target approaches to combination treatments.

The discussion also explored potential therapeutic targets. KRAS mutations, for instance, are present in approximately 90% of pancreatic cancers, making them highly relevant targets, although associated toxicity must be carefully considered due to the role of KRAS in normal cellular functions. Other targets such as BCL-XL and MDM2 were also discussed as promising but challenging candidates. Dr. Touroutoglou expressed interest in the idea of harnessing MDM2 as a degrader, and suggested that such an approach could have therapeutic potential, although toxicity would need to be thoroughly evaluated. He also encouraged us to further investigate EGFR as a target, noting that it is widely expressed in patients and accessible at the cell surface, making it potentially suitable for alternative targeting strategies.

Towards the end of the meeting, Dr. Touroutoglou invited some members of our team, preferably those with a medical background, to visit his office at the European Interbalkan Medical Center in order to gain firsthand exposure to the clinical management of lung cancer and engage directly with patients about their experiences and treatment journeys.

Implementation

This meeting was instrumental in aligning our project with real clinical challenges. It highlighted the importance of addressing tumor heterogeneity, resistance mechanisms, and toxicity, while also reinforcing the need for combination or multi-target approaches. Based on these insights, we will further investigate clinically relevant targets such as KRAS, EGFR, and BCL2-related pathways, evaluate the potential of PROTAC-based strategies, and incorporate clinical data from ongoing trials into our design process. By integrating this perspective, we aim to ensure that our approach is not only scientifically innovative but also clinically meaningful and applicable.

Dr. Konstantinos Porpodis, Dr. Sofia Lampaki

NSCLC Therapies: Second-Line Challenges, Delivery Routes & Patient Acceptance

Stakeholder Profile

Dr. Sofia Lampaki is the Head of the Oncology Clinical Trials Department of the Pulmonology Clinic at the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (AUTH). She also works at the Chemotherapy Day Care Unit of the G. Papanikolaou General Hospital and at the Clinical Trials Department of the Second Oncology Clinic of Euromedica General Clinic of Thessaloniki.

Dr. Konstantinos Porpodis is an Assistant Professor of Pulmonology at the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki and a practicing pulmonologist with expertise in lung cancer diagnosis, treatment, and clinical research. His work focuses on thoracic oncology, respiratory diseases, and the integration of novel therapeutic approaches into clinical practice.

Discussion Summary

We met with Dr. Lampaki and Dr. Porpodis to better understand the current clinical landscape of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the challenges associated with treatment resistance, and the potential acceptance of innovative therapeutic approaches by both clinicians and patients. The discussion focused on treatment pathways, mechanisms of resistance, administration routes, unmet clinical needs, and the willingness of patients to participate in clinical research.

Insights & Recommendations

The clinicians explained that approximately 1,200 new lung cancer cases are diagnosed annually in their clinical setting, with the majority being NSCLC. One of the most significant challenges in lung cancer treatment is the eventual development of resistance to therapy. While resistance mechanisms associated with EGFR-targeted therapies have been extensively studied, many other resistance pathways remain poorly understood and continue to be investigated. They emphasized that treatment resistance is practically inevitable for most patients and represents one of the most pressing problems in NSCLC management.

While first-line therapies can often maintain disease control for up to three years, patients eventually develop resistance and require second-line treatment options. Currently, these options are limited, frequently involving chemotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy regimens that often provide only modest improvements in progression-free survival. As a result, much of the current effort in targeted oncology research focuses on overcoming resistance and expanding second-line treatment opportunities.

The clinicians suggested that EGFR represents a particularly attractive target because its resistance mechanisms have been studied more extensively than those of many other pathways. This makes EGFR-associated resistance a clinically relevant and well-characterized area in which innovative therapeutic approaches could have a meaningful impact.

Regarding safety considerations, they noted that cytotoxicity alone should not necessarily discourage the development of multi-target therapeutic approaches. In clinical practice, prophylactic supportive treatments are routinely administered to vulnerable and elderly patients to mitigate treatment-related toxicities. Therefore, the potential benefits of a more effective therapy may outweigh concerns about moderate increases in cytotoxicity, provided that toxicity remains manageable.

The discussion also addressed administration routes. According to their experience, targeted therapies are most commonly administered orally, while immunotherapies and hormone-based treatments are frequently delivered intravenously. Inhaled therapies, although theoretically attractive for lung cancer, are generally considered difficult to implement successfully in clinical practice. Consequently, intravenous or subcutaneous administration would likely be more realistic options for a novel biologic therapeutic platform such as the one we are proposing.

Beyond the scientific aspects, the clinicians provided valuable insight into patient perspectives. They emphasized that lung cancer patients are generally receptive to experimental therapies and participation in clinical trials, particularly when recommendations come from their treating physician and when there is credible evidence suggesting potential clinical benefit. They did not identify significant barriers regarding patient acceptance or communication, highlighting instead that trust in the treating medical team and confidence in the expected outcomes are the most important factors influencing participation.

Implementation

This discussion significantly influenced our project by reinforcing treatment resistance as one of the most important unmet needs in NSCLC. Based on this feedback, we prioritized targets associated with clinically relevant resistance mechanisms. The clinicians' perspective also encouraged us to focus on therapeutic strategies that could eventually address the limitations of second-line treatment options. Furthermore, their comments regarding cytotoxicity reassured us that a multi-target approach may remain clinically acceptable if it provides meaningful therapeutic benefit. Finally, their insights into patient attitudes confirmed that innovative therapies are likely to be welcomed by patients when supported by strong scientific evidence and physician guidance, further motivating us to pursue a translationally relevant solution.

Ms. Sofia Moysiadou

Exploring Cancer Cell Persistence & KRAS Resistance in NSCLC

Stakeholder Profile

Sofia Moysiadou is a PhD student in the Obenauf Group at the Research Institute of Molecular Pathology (IMP) and a recipient of the prestigious DOC Fellowship from the Austrian Academy of Sciences (ÖAW). She holds a Bachelor's degree in Biology from the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki and a Master's degree in Biomedical Sciences from Utrecht University. Her research focuses on the mechanisms by which lung cancer cells emerge, evolve, and persist during treatment with KRAS inhibitors to acquire therapy resistance. Her expertise offers critical technical and clinical relevance to our project, particularly in understanding cancer cell persistence, optimizing targeted KRAS therapies, and developing strategies to overcome drug resistance.

Discussion Summary

During the meeting, we presented our project idea, focusing on targeting KRAS as one of two primary targets for our peptide PROTAC, along with the proposed experimental design. The team initiated the discussion around the biological barriers driving KRAS inhibitor failure in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), debating whether targeted PROTAC degradation surpasses traditional inhibition in overcoming resistance. Central questions explored Ms. Moysiadou's research and the application of CaTCHseq to trace persistent cell origins, E3 ligase expression stability, escape pathway preferences, p53 signaling dynamics, membrane uptake changes for peptide delivery and microenvironmental factors or acquired vulnerabilities unique to drug-tolerant persistent cells. Main topics of discussion centered on inhibitor efficacy, mechanisms of resistance and insights from Ms. Moysiadou's experimental model in persistent cells.

Insights & Recommendations

Ms. Moysiadou highlighted that both inhibitor efficacy and the development of therapeutic resistance are heavily dependent on the specific compound utilized, noting that high resistance levels are frequently observed in vitro for both ON- and OFF-state KRAS inhibitors.

Moreover, a key insight from her research reshaped our team's understanding of resistance mechanisms: while we initially focused on drug-induced adaptations, Ms. Moysiadou emphasized that therapeutic resistance, particularly in KRAS-targeted lung cancer therapies, is primarily driven by pre-existing cellular features rather than solely de novo mutations acquired during treatment.

Furthermore, she addressed a core question central to our project's value proposition: whether targeted protein degradation via PROTACs offers a superior therapeutic efficacy over traditional small-molecule inhibition. Ms. Moysiadou validated our team's hypothesis and literature findings, agreeing that PROTAC-mediated degradation provides greater efficacy. While drug-tolerant persistent cells inevitably emerge across all current KRAS-targeted therapies, PROTAC-based approaches delay this onset, causing resistance to develop substantially later compared to conventional inhibition.

Regarding her ongoing research, long-term cell line models maintained under inhibitor treatment for up to 130 days demonstrated that drug-tolerant persistent cells survive primarily through pre-existing primed states rather than acquired biochemical rewiring. When the primary driver (e.g., KRAS) is blocked, these primed cells evade death by leveraging pre-established backup pathways to reroute their survival signaling. Alternatively, cells persist by slowing down their overall metabolic and biosynthetic activity to enter a dormant, "sleep-like" state. Because this dormant survival mode relies on energy conservation rather than radical pathway reconfiguration, the metabolomic profiles of these persistent cells remain remarkably similar to baseline, non-resistant cells.

The discussion highlighted several biological hurdles that could impact our therapeutic approach. A major concern is that persistent cancer cells leverage pre-existing survival networks; when the primary target (KRAS) is degraded or inhibited, cells can divert survival signals through these alternative pathways. Furthermore, in vivo microenvironmental factors, such as altered cytokine secretion, play a pivotal role in promoting cell persistence that cannot be fully replicated in standard 2D in vitro models. Lastly, potential variations in E3 ligase expression or membrane dynamics during drug-induced dormancy could alter the uptake efficacy of peptide-based PROTACs compared to traditional small molecules. Fortunately, we ensured through literature review that the E3 ligase recruited by our PROTAC system is abundantly expressed in our chosen cell line model.

Insights & Recommendations

The discussion emphasized that a proper biorisk assessment should be approached in two distinct stages: evaluating risks to the environment and evaluating risks to the patient. Environmental risks include the possibility that the therapeutic compound, delivery system, or excreted materials could negatively affect ecosystems or individuals exposed to them. However, it was noted that such risks are generally limited because therapeutic compounds are typically released in very low concentrations. Patient-related risks, on the other hand, are much more significant, since side effects and off-target activity can directly harm healthy tissues and compromise treatment safety.

Particular attention was given to categories of research that require especially strict biosecurity considerations, although these do not directly apply to our project. These include the genetic modification of cells and the use of viruses or viral components that could evolve or spread into the environment, potentially affecting broader populations or ecosystems such as livestock and poultry. Since our project does not involve pathogenic organisms or replication-competent viral systems, the overall risk profile was considered relatively low. Nevertheless, we were advised that the safety of each component should still be evaluated carefully through literature review and by examining existing assessments of compounds with similar profiles.

A major recommendation from the discussion was to maintain the lowest possible biosafety level during experimental work, ideally Biosafety Level 1, and to avoid the use of high-risk pathogens or unnecessary biological hazards. It was noted that oncology-related systems are generally based on non-pathogenic materials and are therefore typically considered safe when proper laboratory practices are followed. We were specifically advised never to use our own cells in experimental procedures. Instead, certified and well-characterized laboratory cell lines should always be employed.

The importance of laboratory culture and preparation was another key topic. Experimental protocols should be thoroughly planned in advance and reviewed by multiple individuals from different scientific backgrounds in order to identify possible weaknesses or overlooked risks. We also discussed whether the nanoparticles or RNA constructs could remain active or toxic in the environment and whether their biological behavior differs between human cells and environmental systems. The safety profile of mRNA therapeutics was also discussed. It was emphasized that therapeutic activity should not be permanent, and that ideally a "kill switch" or other control mechanism should exist in case severe adverse effects occur.

Off-target degradation or inhibition could unintentionally disrupt normal cellular functions, making selectivity one of the most critical parameters of our design. At the same time, it was noted that the majority of biosecurity protection in projects like ours comes not from the intrinsic danger of the materials, but from the consistent application of rigorous biosafety measures and responsible laboratory practices. Exploring High-Risk Demographics: Occupational Carcinogen Exposure in Firefighting

Stakeholder Profile

Mr. Bikas serves as a Senior Officer of the Hellenic Fire Corps and is an Expert Officer of the European Civil Protection Mechanism. His extensive fields of expertise include firefighting operations, occupational hazard exposure, and environmental and fire safety issues of companies. Engaging with him was of high societal and clinical relevance to our project, which focuses on lung cancer. Firefighters represent a highly vulnerable demographic exposed to intense, complex airborne carcinogens, making his perspective crucial for understanding the real-world conditions, chemical exposures, and behavioral factors that contribute to respiratory diseases in this profession.

Discussion Summary

The iGEM Thessaloniki team presented our project's core focus on lung cancer and sought to understand the reality of occupational exposure for firefighters. Because we did not want to rely solely on literature, we asked Mr. Bikas about the daily challenges, the types of chemical environments his colleagues face, and the practical use of safety equipment. The discussion centered on the variability of pollutants depending on the type of fire, specifically contrasting urban and forest fires. We also explored the behavioral psychology of firefighters during emergencies and how it impacts health and safety protocols, the long-term health consequences observed within the firefighting community, and the limitations of empirical observation versus clinical data.

Insights & Recommendations

Mr. Bikas provided invaluable insights into the realities of firefighting, highlighting both chemical and behavioral hazards. He explained that the composition of inhaled pollutants depends entirely on what is burning. While carbon monoxide is a primary hazard, especially in enclosed spaces, it is diluted in forest fires and poses a slightly lower immediate toxic threat. Conversely, in urban fires involving houses or vehicles, firefighters are heavily exposed to highly toxic compounds like phenols and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) byproducts.

A profound psychological insight he shared was the "immediate action" dilemma. Firefighters often neglect health precautions in the heat of the moment, driven by the urge to act immediately and save lives, sometimes entering hazardous environments without their respiratory equipment. Despite this instinct to rush in unprotected, Mr. Bikas noted that the proper use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) actually enables more effective and immediate action in the long run, though this mindset remains hard to enforce in a crisis. Consequently, he confirmed that older, veteran firefighters suffer from significant respiratory and cardiovascular problems, probably due to repeated and long-term exposure. Finally, Mr. Bikas noted that his observations are empirical and based on field experience and pointed out that firefighter deaths are rarely researched.

Implementation

The feedback from Mr. Bikas actively shaped our Integrated Human Practices and our project's strategic direction. The meeting confirmed firefighters as a primary target group for our project, reinforcing its societal relevance. We realized that any therapeutic tool we design could have a massive societal impact on this specific, high-risk community.
