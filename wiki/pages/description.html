    A Global Burden

    Lung cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in the world, with about 2.6 million new cases in 2024. Since non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) makes up roughly 85% of all lung cancers, that translates to well over 2 million new NSCLC cases every year. The sheer scale of the disease — spread across every region of the globe — is what makes it one of the most urgent targets in oncology today.

    The Deadliest Cancer

    Lung cancer is not only the most common cancer but also the leading cause of cancer death worldwide, responsible for an estimated 1.9 million deaths in 2024 — 19.1% of all cancer deaths. That is roughly double the next cause, colorectal cancer (9.4%), and more than liver (7.5%), breast (7.1%), and stomach (6.6%) cancers. Its high share of deaths relative to diagnoses reflects how poor outcomes remain, underscoring the need for earlier detection and better therapies.

    An Uneven Global Toll

    Lung cancer mortality is not distributed evenly across the world. Using the age-standardized mortality rate — which adjusts for population size and age so regions can be compared fairly — the death rate is highest in Europe and Asia and lowest in Africa, a difference of more than four-fold. These disparities largely track historical smoking patterns, industrialization, and access to healthcare, showing that lung cancer is shaped by environment and policy as much as biology.

    Caught Too Late

    This chart shows the stage at which lung cancer is actually diagnosed. Only about 1 in 5 cases (22%) is caught early, while it is still localized to the lung; more than half (53%) are found only after the cancer has already spread to distant organs — the stage with under 10% five-year survival. This late-detection gap is one of the central reasons lung cancer remains so lethal, and it is precisely the problem that better diagnostic and therapeutic tools aim to close.

    The Threat of Recurrence

    Even patients whose tumors are surgically removed with the intent to cure are not out of danger. The 5-year recurrence rate climbs steadily with stage at surgery — from around 29% in stage IA to nearly 60% by stage IIB — and most relapses appear at distant sites, where they are far harder to treat. This shows that surgery alone is often not enough, motivating research into monitoring and more precise therapy.

    Subtypes & Clinical Presentation

    From a histopathological perspective, lung adenocarcinoma arises from a precancerous lesion known as Atypical Adenomatous Hyperplasia (AAH), most frequently located in the peripheral lung parenchyma. As cytologic atypia within the AAH lesion increases, it gives rise to Adenocarcinoma in Situ (AIS), the first distinct malignant lesion in this sequence. AIS is a localized lesion ≤3 cm showing pure lepidic growth along intact alveolar septa, without stromal, vascular, or pleural invasion, and is subdivided into Mucinous and Non-Mucinous AIS. As neoplastic cells accumulate further genetic alterations, focal invasion appears and the lesion becomes Minimally Invasive Adenocarcinoma (MIA) — a predominantly lepidic adenocarcinoma ≤3 cm with an invasive focus ≤5 mm, again subdivided into Mucinous and Non-Mucinous. Finally, once the invasive component enlarges beyond 5 mm — or shows vascular invasion, pleural invasion, or spread through air spaces (STAS) — it is reclassified as Invasive Adenocarcinoma, which is histologically divided into seven subtypes.

    Composed of tall columnar cells with abundant intracytoplasmic mucin and basally located nuclei, typically growing in a lepidic, acinar, or papillary pattern; it tends to spread multicentrically within the lung and often produces copious mucin.

    Can be further subdivided into five distinct histological growth patterns, with the micropapillary and solid patterns associated with a worse prognosis, reflecting more aggressive biological behavior.

    • Lepidic: Tumor cells line pre-existing alveolar walls without invading the underlying stroma, vasculature, or pleura, preserving native lung architecture; the least aggressive pattern with the best prognosis.
    • Acinar: Tumor cells form round to oval glands with a central lumen, lined by cuboidal to columnar cells in a fibrous stroma; the most commonly encountered pattern.
    • Papillary: Tumor cells grow along branching fibrovascular cores that project into alveolar spaces, producing a finger-like architecture.
    • Micropapillary: Tumor cells form small papillary tufts lacking fibrovascular cores that appear to "float" within alveolar spaces; strongly associated with lymphovascular invasion, early metastasis, and higher recurrence risk.
    • Solid: Tumor cells grow in sheets or nests with no discernible glandular architecture; mucin stains or immunohistochemistry are often needed to confirm adenocarcinoma lineage.

    Tumors containing both a mucinous and a non-mucinous component, each comprising a significant proportion of the tumor, reflecting divergent differentiation within a single neoplasm.

    Characterized by large pools of extracellular mucin that distend and destroy the alveolar spaces, with scant clusters of bland tumor cells floating within the mucin lakes or lining the alveolar walls.

    Composed of glands resembling fetal lung tubules, lined by glycogen-rich, non-mucinous cells with sub- and supranuclear glycogen vacuolation, often featuring morules (squamoid nests).

    Resembles colorectal adenocarcinoma both morphologically (glandular/cribriform architecture with necrosis) and immunohistochemically.

    Reserved for poorly differentiated tumors that show glandular differentiation by morphology, mucin stains, or immunohistochemistry, but lack a recognizable specific growth pattern, precluding classification into one of the other subtypes.
