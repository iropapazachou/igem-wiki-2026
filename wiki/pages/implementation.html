    From Our Prototype to a Real-World Therapy

    Why think about implementation at this stage?

    Developing a therapeutic concept is not only about demonstrating that it works in a laboratory. A molecule can successfully degrade its target in vitro and still fail to become a viable treatment because it cannot reach the tumour, cannot be manufactured consistently, is unstable during storage, causes unacceptable toxicity, or cannot be administered effectively.

    For this reason, implementation has been an integral part of AETh3R from the beginning, since we are designing a potential therapeutic system that must eventually address the entire path from molecular design to patient treatment. Early consideration of implementation allows us to identify potential translational barriers before investing in a prototype that may be difficult or impossible to develop further.

    What problems does our product address?

    Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treatment has advanced considerably through targeted therapies and immunotherapies, yet treatment resistance and disease progression remain important clinical challenges. These clinical barriers have consistently driven our project design to ensure optimal translatability (see our Description page):

    • AETh3R explores targeted protein degradation as an alternative therapeutic modality with the potential to address key resistance mechanisms that disarm conventional inhibitors.
    • Combination treatment targeting KRAS and MDM2 is a direct response to clinical insight and expert feedback highlighting combinatorial therapies as a necessary response to cancer adaptability and polyclonality, a core issue in NSCLC.
    • Our targeted lipid nanoparticle delivery system addresses one of the central translational challenges of peptide-based intracellular therapeutics: selectively sequestering the active molecule at its site of action, while sparing healthy tissues.

    Key implementation milestones

    Thinking about implementation early allows us to define what evidence would actually be needed for AETh3R to progress beyond proof of concept. We therefore envision AETh3R developing through the following stages, each generating information that feeds back into the therapeutic design and guides its iterative evolution.

    1. Therapeutic design. Selection and optimisation of the peptide PROTAC, target and E3 ligase-recruitment strategy.
    2. Delivery optimisation. Development of an LNP capable of efficiently encapsulating, protecting and delivering the peptide.
    3. In vitro proof of concept. Demonstration of cellular uptake, target engagement, target degradation and downstream antitumour effects.
    4. Formulation optimisation. Optimisation of potency, stability, reproducibility and other relevant physicochemical properties.
    5. Preclinical development. Evaluation of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, pharmacodynamics, efficacy and toxicity in appropriate models.
    6. Manufacturing development. Establishment of scalable and reproducible production under appropriate pharmaceutical quality standards.
    7. Clinical development. Progression through Phase I, II and III clinical trials to establish safety, dosing and efficacy.
    8. Continued evaluation. Long-term monitoring of safety, efficacy, resistance and opportunities for further formulation improvement.

    Safety, Ethics & Responsible Implementation

    In our research project on NSCLC, we are developing a therapeutic concept that combines targeted protein degradation with nanoparticle-based delivery. While our approach aims to address important limitations of existing cancer therapies, we recognise that developing a new therapeutic strategy also introduces important questions regarding patient safety, biosafety, ethics, environmental responsibility and regulatory compliance. For this reason, responsible implementation has been a fundamental consideration from the beginning, rather than a requirement to be addressed only once our prototype is complete.

    We believe that scientific progress must be accompanied by integrity, transparency and accountability. Throughout the development of AETh3R, we have therefore considered not only whether our proposed therapeutic could work, but also the conditions under which it could be developed and eventually used responsibly. This has guided our decisions regarding experimental design, laboratory safety, the selection of appropriate models, potential risks associated with our delivery system, and the evidence that would be required before any future transition towards clinical development.

    For our team, safety and ethics are therefore not separate from scientific progress, but part of what defines successful research. AETh3R should only move forward when the evidence, safety considerations and ethical framework are strong enough to justify the next step.

    From the Lab to the Patient

    Defining Our Target Market

    Lung cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, accounting for 12.4% of all global cancer cases, with nearly 2.5 million new cases and over 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022 [1]. NSCLC constitutes the vast majority of these cases, representing 80–85% of all diagnoses. As the primary driver of global cancer morbidity and mortality, lung cancer was responsible for an estimated 1.9 million deaths in 2024, meaning nearly one in five (19.1%) cancer deaths worldwide [2]. Its annual mortality exceeds that of colon, breast and prostate cancers combined.

    While ranking first in men and second in women globally for incidence and mortality, lung cancer shows striking geographic variation in its gender gap. Male-to-female ratios range from roughly 1:1 in Northern America and Northern Europe to as high as 5:1 in Northern Africa and Eastern Europe, largely tracking historical regional tobacco use. By world region, elevated incidence rates are seen in Northern America, Eastern Asia and Northern Europe [1].

    The disease predominantly affects older adults, both men and women from ages 50 and older, with a median age at diagnosis of roughly 70 years [3]. Patient prognosis remains sharply dependent on the stage at detection. Because early symptoms are often subtle, only 22% of cases are identified while localised; conversely, over half (53%) are diagnosed after distant metastasis, where 5-year survival drops below 10% [4]. This late-stage bias underscores an imperative need for early, high-sensitivity diagnostic and screening modalities. Even among patients undergoing surgical resection, 5-year recurrence rates escalate from ~29% in stage IA to nearly 60% in stage IIB, with relapses predominantly occurring as distant metastases [5].

    NSCLC mortality has dropped significantly over recent years due to declines in smoking rates alongside major therapeutic advances. Yet it remains exceptionally difficult to treat in advanced, recurrent or metastatic stages. High intratumoral heterogeneity drives differential treatment response across tumour subregions, fuelling drug resistance, distant relapse and disease progression. This biological complexity is compounded by treatment toxicity, late-stage diagnosis, comorbidities, high costs and socioeconomic barriers to care, alongside a heavy physical and emotional symptom burden. These multidimensional challenges underscore an urgent unmet need for novel, highly selective therapeutic modalities that can overcome resistance mechanisms, improve survival and minimise systemic toxicity and treatment burden, while delivering favourable safety profiles, cost-effective production and scalable manufacturing feasibility.

    The global NSCLC market represents a substantial commercial and clinical opportunity driven by high disease incidence and evolving precision medicine paradigms. With NSCLC representing ~85% of all cases [6], global incidence stands at roughly 2 million new patients per year [7], defining the Total Addressable Market (TAM). Financially, the NSCLC therapeutics market across the seven major markets (US, EU4, UK, Japan) was valued at approximately USD 33 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach USD 67 billion by 2036 [8], with broader estimates for all global lung cancer therapeutics spanning USD 46.7 billion in 2026 to USD 141.2 billion by 2036 [9].

    Refining this landscape to a molecularly defined Serviceable Available Market (SAM), KRAS mutations occur in 25–35% of newly diagnosed NSCLC cases overall [10], reaching 37.2–40% in large adenocarcinoma cohorts depending on demographic factors [11][12]. Additionally, MDM2 amplification is observed in 3.5–7% of tumours in large NGS pan-cancer datasets [13][14] and up to 21% in surgical cohorts [15]. Applying these molecular prevalence rates to the total annual incidence yields a SAM of approximately 500,000–700,000 patients per year globally [7][10][11][14][15].

    Within this addressable landscape, AETh3R's Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) focuses on advanced, resistant or refractory NSCLC patients unserved by current standard-of-care options. Currently approved KRAS inhibitors (sotorasib, adagrasib) exclusively target the G12C mutation [16], which accounts for only ~40% of KRAS mutations in NSCLC [11]. This leaves ~60% of KRAS-mutated patients, specifically those harbouring G12D/V and other non-G12C variants, without an approved targeted therapy [16]. Moreover, even among G12C-treated patients, response rates remain limited due to intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms [17]. By targeting non-G12C KRAS variants and MDM2-altered tumours in the advanced setting, AETh3R's realistic launch SOM captures a global population in the low hundreds of thousands of patients annually [11][16][17], unlocking a multi-billion-dollar commercial opportunity within the targeted NSCLC landscape.

    Who Are the End Users?

    Our final product is a novel, dual-targeting therapeutic platform comprising de novo peptide-PROTACs (p-PROTACs) that simultaneously target two critical oncogenic drivers in NSCLC: mutant KRAS and MDM2. The target user base and commercial ecosystem for AETh3R span three distinct operational tiers.

    1. Primary tier. The direct business-to-business (B2B) customers are biopharmaceutical and biotechnology companies specialising in oncology and targeted protein degradation (TPD). Because early-stage drug discovery is increasingly outsourced or out-licensed to bypass small-molecule limitations and degrade previously "undruggable" targets such as mutant KRAS and MDM2, these biopharma partners would in-license the p-PROTAC candidates to conduct downstream wet-lab optimisation, preclinical validation, clinical trials and regulatory filings.
    2. Secondary tier. Biomanufacturing organisations, Contract Development and Manufacturing Organisations (CDMOs) and commercial-stage pharmaceutical partners license the IP, production rights and biomanufacturing know-how from AETh3R to scale manufacturing and distribute the final therapeutic through specialised healthcare supply chains.
    3. Clinical tier. Once the therapy receives regulatory approval and hospital integration, the direct clinical users are specialised oncology care teams, including medical oncologists, molecular pathologists and clinical pharmacists. The end users are patients with NSCLC who would be administered the treatment following medical recommendation.

    From Production to Patient

    Manufacturing & production conditions

    The final AETh3R product would combine a peptide PROTAC with an LNP delivery system. Its production would therefore involve several interconnected stages, all of which would need to follow appropriate Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards. The peptide component would require controlled synthesis, purification and structural characterisation. The LNP formulation would require controlled manufacturing of parameters such as lipid composition, peptide-to-lipid ratio, particle size, polydispersity, surface charge, peptide loading and encapsulation efficiency, with a reproducible scale-up method such as microfluidic mixing [18].

    Peptide synthesis → purification → characterisation

    LNP formulation → purification / buffer exchange

    → sterile formulation → quality control → storage → distribution

    However, a formulation that performs successfully at laboratory scale cannot automatically be transferred to pharmaceutical-scale production. Scale-up would therefore require the manufacturing process to maintain the physicochemical and biological characteristics of the formulation while increasing production volume. Process parameters would need to be optimised, controlled and validated before clinical manufacturing.

    Batch-to-batch consistency would be essential to ensure that patients receive a product with predictable characteristics. For that reason, the final product would need predefined critical quality attributes (CQAs) such as particle size and size distribution, polydispersity, surface charge, peptide loading and encapsulation efficiency, peptide integrity, lipid composition, purity, aggregation, sterility, endotoxin levels, potency and target degradation activity.

    Delivery & maintenance

    The final storage conditions would be established experimentally through comprehensive stability studies assessing peptide and LNP integrity, aggregation, temperature sensitivity, freeze–thaw stability and robustness during transportation. Based on the storage requirements of currently available LNP-based therapeutics, maintaining a cold chain would likely be necessary [19]. However, the appropriate storage temperature and shelf life cannot be defined at this stage and would only be established following sufficient stability data to demonstrate product integrity and performance under the proposed conditions.

    Dose optimisation & administration

    The therapeutic dose cannot be determined directly from an in vitro concentration. Instead, dose selection would progress from cellular experiments to animal studies and finally to human clinical trials. The objective would be to identify a dose that produces sufficient target degradation and therapeutic activity while remaining within an acceptable safety margin. Repeated dosing would be required, with pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamic activity informing the intervals between subsequent administrations, and the appropriate dosing schedule depending on in vivo data and the individual response of patients to the drug.

    The administration route would be selected according to the pharmacokinetic, biodistribution and safety characteristics of the final formulation. Intravenous administration could provide controlled systemic exposure and is a robust potential route for an initial formulation. Pulmonary administration could also be investigated in some cases; however, this route introduces additional challenges, including cough reflexes, mucus barriers, lung clearance, particle deposition and dose reproducibility, as well as a strong dependence on tumour localisation within the lung or peripheral tissue metastases — which is why we and the stakeholders we consulted have opted against it [20].

    Risks, Limitations & Safety Profile

    Comparative Safety and Risk Model

    In the absence of clinical data, evaluating worst-case safety scenarios is vital for guiding Investigational New Drug (IND)-enabling preclinical studies and establishing safety margins. Because our dual p-PROTAC therapeutic approach combines MDM2 and KRAS degradation with a targeted lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery system with integrin αvβ6 targeting, its safety profile reflects overlapping biological mechanisms.

    To construct a comprehensive risk model, we analysed clinical safety data across adjacent classes: clinical-stage MDM2 antagonists, FDA-approved KRAS inhibitors, LNP-formulated therapeutics and the first FDA-approved PROTAC.

    Integrated Worst-Case Scenario Analysis

    In a hypothetical worst-case safety scenario, AETh3R could exhibit overlapping toxicities associated with its therapeutic components and lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery platform. Pharmacological disruption of the MDM2–p53 pathway has been associated with haematological and gastrointestinal toxicities in clinical studies of MDM2 inhibitors, supporting concern that p53 pathway activation in healthy proliferative tissues could limit tolerability. However, the safety profile of MDM2 degradation may differ from that of MDM2 inhibition and would require direct evaluation. Similarly, systemic KRAS depletion could potentially disrupt normal cellular functions, although hepatic, renal and pulmonary injury should be regarded as unconfirmed risks rather than established consequences of KRAS degradation. Depending on its composition, the LNP delivery platform may introduce additional risks, including infusion-related reactions, complement activation, inflammatory responses and anti-PEG immune responses. Because targeted protein degraders eliminate target proteins rather than merely inhibiting specific functional sites, the biological effect of AETh3R may also differ from that of conventional inhibitors, making assessment of degradation selectivity and unintended target loss important.

    The combination of these potential liabilities could create a cumulative toxicity burden if AETh3R reaches healthy tissues at exposures sufficient to cause substantial target degradation. Nevertheless, additive or synergistic toxicity across tissues has not been established for AETh3R and should be treated as a hypothesis for preclinical investigation. These possibilities establish the principal safety parameters that must be controlled during dose escalation: where the therapeutic accumulates, how much target degradation occurs, and how long that activity persists.

    However, AETh3R is specifically engineered to shift this toxicity landscape away from the broad systemic exposure characteristic of conventional therapies.

    1. Antibody-guided LNP targeting. Antibody-guided LNP targeting preferentially directs the therapeutic payload toward NSCLC tissue, reducing non-specific exposure of healthy organs and thereby decreasing the probability that MDM2 or KRAS degradation will occur at clinically relevant levels outside the tumour. This spatial restriction directly addresses a central limitation of conventional systemic therapy: the inability to sufficiently distinguish tumour tissue from healthy tissue during drug distribution. It is particularly important for MDM2, where systemic p53 activation can damage healthy bone marrow and intestinal epithelium, and for KRAS, where systemic pathway inhibition can interfere with normal tissue homeostasis.
    2. Selective and catalytic mechanism of p-PROTAC-mediated degradation. Unlike conventional inhibitors that require sustained systemic concentrations to maintain target suppression, p-PROTACs can repeatedly engage their targets and achieve substantial protein depletion without requiring continuous high concentrations of therapeutic molecules. Achieving robust protein knockdown at lower doses lowers the risk of off-target toxicity, expanding the therapeutic index while lessening the overall systemic burden tied to persistent target occupancy. Following clearance of the active therapeutic, newly synthesised target protein can restore protein abundance over time, providing an additional opportunity to manage toxicity through dose, treatment interval and treatment interruption. Furthermore, peptide-based protein–protein interaction recognition may enable engagement of broader interaction surfaces and improve target-binding selectivity, potentially limiting unintended protein interactions and off-target degradation [X1].
    3. Optimised LNP dynamics. AETh3R's LNP architecture incorporates inherent lung tropism via optimised formulation dynamics, with the addition of the cationic SORT (Selective ORgan Targeting) molecule DOTAP, alongside optimised PEG surface density and chains aimed at controlling PEG exposure and associated immune recognition. Combined with antibody-guided tumour targeting, this design is intended to reduce systemic inflammatory and immunogenic liabilities associated with conventional LNP administration, including complement activation and anti-PEG responses. This dual organ- and cell-level spatial restriction provides an additional layer of control over the tolerability of the delivery platform, particularly during repeated administration [35].

    Consequently, the central safety advantage of AETh3R lies not in eliminating individual toxicities, but in controlling where, how much and for how long therapeutic activity occurs. By preferentially concentrating a transient, catalytic degradation event within target NSCLC tissue, reducing the requirement for sustained systemic exposure, and optimising the tolerability of the delivery vehicle, AETh3R is designed to widen the separation between antitumour activity and healthy-tissue toxicity. This creates a therapeutic framework in which dose, treatment interval and targeting efficiency can be strategically optimised to maximise tumour KRAS and MDM2 depletion while limiting systemic toxicity, resulting in a safety profile that is expected to differ meaningfully from conventional systemic MDM2 or KRAS inhibition.

    Finally, it is essential to note that certain adverse events observed in benchmark drug classes may be driven by chemical motifs, excipients or off-target profiles specific to those individual agents that are absent in our formulation. On the other hand, first-in-human clinical evaluation of AETh3R may reveal novel emergent side effects not captured in this preliminary comparative model. Nevertheless, this theoretical assessment provides a structured benchmark for guiding IND-enabling toxicology studies, establishing safe clinical starting doses and protecting participant safety during early-phase development.

    Residual Safety Risks & AETh3R Vulnerabilities

    MPS clearance

    AETh3R's antibody-guided targeting is designed to preferentially direct LNPs toward target-expressing NSCLC tissue, and it also incorporates controlled surface density and rapidly cleavable short-anchor PEG chains to minimise protein corona formation, limit hepatic exposure and improve repeat-dose tolerability. However, while AETh3R is engineered to avoid non-specific clearance, systemic circulation inevitably exposes a fraction of the formulation to physiological clearance mechanisms. The liver's fenestrated vasculature drives dominant uptake via mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) Kupffer cells. Upon systemic contact, LNPs rapidly adsorb plasma proteins to form a protein corona, marking them for phagocytic clearance regardless of targeting motifs.

    This residual exposure poses safety considerations, as hepatic LNP accumulation can cause transient transaminase elevations and inflammatory responses, while repeat dosing increases anti-PEG immune recognition. These risks do not negate the targeting strategy; rather, they define the residual systemic exposure that the design must control [36].

    Modality-specific vulnerabilities

    Despite the development of engineered solutions aimed at mitigating adverse effects, the p-PROTAC modality retains intrinsic safety vulnerabilities and residual toxicological risks inherent to its mechanism and structure.

    • Off-target binding. Although PROTACs are designed for selective target degradation, cross-reactivity or promiscuous binding can induce the degradation of unintended off-target proteins, resulting in tissue-specific toxicity, differential organ responses, or other adverse reactions that compromise their safety profile.
    • UPS over-saturation. Because the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) has a finite processing capacity, excessive activation or saturation of the proteasome with abundant substrates can overwhelm cellular degradation pathways, triggering cytotoxic cellular stress, activating compensatory pathways and ultimately compromising cell viability.
    • Systemic administration. Potential immunogenicity, particularly with peptidic moieties, can trigger adverse immune reactions, accelerate clearance from circulation and diminish exposure at target sites.
    • Drug–drug interactions. Co-administration with other medications introduces the risk of drug–drug interactions, which can unpredictably alter the metabolism, clearance and safety profiles of both the PROTAC and co-administered compounds.
    • Non-canonical function inhibition. Protein degradation can affect functions beyond a target's canonical catalytic activity. Whereas a conventional inhibitor may block a specific functional site while leaving the protein scaffold intact, complete removal of KRAS or MDM2 may disrupt protein–protein interactions or structural functions within multiprotein complexes. This creates the possibility of broader signalling perturbations that would not necessarily be predicted from inhibitor-based toxicity profiles.
    • Dual-targeting liabilities. The simultaneous and rapid depletion of MDM2 and KRAS creates dual-targeting liabilities: while synergistically amplifying apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest in tumour cells, this co-degradation can trigger excessive overall cytotoxicity and injure normal proliferating tissues if target engagement is not strictly tumour-selective. Because MDM2 and KRAS represent broadly important survival and regulatory pathways, their concurrent degradation risks overlapping on-target toxicities that cannot be independently dose-adjusted, creating a narrow therapeutic window. During dose escalation, these mechanisms heighten the risk that accelerated tumour clearance translates into localised tissue injury, systemic tolerability limits and unpredictable organ-specific toxicities in human translation [37][38][39].

    Importantly, these residual vulnerabilities are precisely the risks that the AETh3R safety strategy is designed to characterise and control through exposure optimisation, target-specificity assessment and tissue-level monitoring.

    Contraindications & patient exclusion criteria

    To ensure patient safety and maintain a favourable risk–benefit profile during clinical evaluation, strict selection boundaries are required for AETh3R. Because its dual-degradation mechanism targets proteins with essential homeostatic functions, indiscriminate systemic exposure or target depletion in healthy tissue poses significant toxicological risks. Consequently, eligibility criteria must account for the physiological necessity of MDM2, KRAS and integrin αvβ6 across normal tissues to prevent severe on-target, off-organ toxicities.

    MDM2 acts as the primary negative regulator of p53, controlling its levels to prevent lethal p53 accumulation and apoptosis in both proliferative progenitors and developing non-proliferating cells during early embryonic development and organogenesis. Without MDM2 regulation, unchecked p53 activity causes widespread cellular toxicity and lethality across both embryonic and postnatal developmental stages [40]. Moreover, KRAS is essential for normal embryonic development around mid-gestation, where its complete loss in nonclinical models causes lethality due to defects in placental development, heart morphogenesis, fetal liver haematopoiesis and neural cell survival, while germline dysregulation in humans causes RASopathies marked by severe congenital cardiac, craniofacial and growth malformations [41][42]. Additionally, integrin αvβ6 contributes to cell–extracellular matrix interactions, tissue remodelling and activation of TGF-β, thereby regulating processes involved in tissue and organ formation and maturation. Its expression has been reported in selected embryonic epithelial tissues, including the developing lung, kidney and skin, as well as in human fetal membranes, particularly the amniotic epithelium [X2][X3][X4].

    Taken together, AETh3R is contraindicated during pregnancy, lactation and early childhood. Furthermore, after examining the established safety profiles of MDM2- and KRAS-targeting agents, we believe that treatment should be contraindicated in patients with hypersensitivity to the active substance or its excipients.

    However, it is important to note that these clinical restrictions currently stem from the established physiological roles of the target proteins and theoretical mechanistic liabilities inherent to dual-degradation platforms. Formal definition and refinement of these boundaries will require verification through experimental data. Finally, secondary clinical boundaries and patient exclusion criteria will be continuously updated as further preclinical and clinical data emerge.

    The Road Ahead

    Developing AETh3R into a potential therapeutic would require much more than demonstrating that our peptide PROTAC can degrade its target in a laboratory setting. Our current work represents the beginning of a longer development pathway in which each stage would provide the evidence needed to determine whether the therapy is suitable for further investigation. We therefore envision a gradual progression from in vitro validation to preclinical evaluation, and eventually to clinical development, with safety, efficacy and feasibility assessed throughout the process.

    Preclinical Development

    In vitro studies

    Our first experimental priority will be to establish whether our therapeutic concept can achieve the intended effect at the cellular level. First, we will characterise and validate the peptide PROTAC itself, assessing its ability to interact with the selected target and promote its degradation. We will evaluate target engagement and degradation through appropriate protein-level assays, while also examining the stability and specificity of the construct. These experiments will allow us to determine whether our molecular design produces the mechanism of action we intend, using a less complicated delivery approach such as Lipofectamine 2000 encapsulation.

    A crucial step will be to investigate how the peptide interacts with both early liposomal delivery constructs and our mature LNP formulation. Since peptide cargoes differ fundamentally from the nucleic acids for which many LNP systems have been developed, efficient association with the nanoparticle cannot simply be assumed. Based on our discussions with Dr. Tsourkas, we will therefore investigate whether the peptide requires the incorporation of a polyanionic tail to facilitate electrostatic complexation with the LNP and improve its encapsulation and delivery. We will compare the relevant peptide designs and characterise parameters such as encapsulation efficiency, particle size, polydispersity, surface charge and peptide stability. This step will help us determine whether the peptide itself can be efficiently incorporated into the formulation or whether an additional structural modification is required.

    Then, we will evaluate the cellular delivery of the complete PROTAC–LNP system, investigating whether the formulation can be taken up by relevant NSCLC cells and whether the peptide is successfully released intracellularly. Appropriate fluorescence-based uptake studies and microscopy or flow-cytometry analyses could be used to distinguish nanoparticle association from effective cellular internalisation, with the split-GFP strategy employed by Dr. Tsourkas and colleagues being a good candidate assay for confirming endosomal escape and cytoplasmic localisation. Importantly, we will also assess the effects of the LNP formulation alone and appropriate control formulations, allowing us to distinguish the biological activity of the peptide PROTAC from potential effects caused by the delivery system itself.

    Next experiments will determine whether successful intracellular delivery translates into the intended therapeutic response. We will measure degradation of the selected target and subsequently investigate downstream effects on NSCLC cell viability, proliferation and apoptosis. Dose-response and time-course experiments will allow us to characterise how the response changes with exposure, while appropriate negative and positive controls will help establish whether the observed effects are target-dependent. We will also compare relevant cancerous and non-target cell models where possible, providing an initial assessment of selectivity and potential off-target toxicity.

    Through this staged approach, we aim to move from molecular validation, to formulation, to intracellular delivery, and finally to biological activity. Each stage will provide information for the next: insufficient degradation could require redesign of the PROTAC, poor peptide–LNP association could motivate the introduction of a polyanionic tail, inadequate cellular uptake could require reformulation of the LNP, and nonspecific toxicity could indicate the need to modify either the therapeutic construct or its delivery system. In this way, our in vitro experiments will not only demonstrate whether AETh3R works, but will help us understand which components of the system need to be optimised before progressing towards in vivo evaluation [37].

    In vivo studies

    If AETh3R demonstrates sufficient activity and selectivity in vitro, the next stage would be to determine whether these properties can be maintained within a living organism. Appropriate animal models would be selected to investigate how the formulation behaves in vivo and whether it can reach the tissues relevant to NSCLC.

    Initial studies would focus on biodistribution and pharmacokinetics, allowing us to determine where the LNP travels, how long the therapeutic remains available, and whether the intended target tissues can be reached. Pharmacodynamic studies would then examine whether delivery results in the expected target degradation in vivo.

    These studies would be accompanied by assessments of therapeutic efficacy and toxicity. We would investigate whether the treatment can produce measurable antitumour effects while maintaining an acceptable safety profile, including evaluation of potential systemic toxicity, immune responses and effects on non-target tissues. Any animal studies would only be undertaken following the appropriate ethical and regulatory approvals and in accordance with the principles of the 3Rs.

    The results of these studies would help determine whether AETh3R has sufficient evidence to justify progression towards clinical development. They could also reveal limitations that require us to return to earlier stages of the project, for example by modifying the PROTAC structure, reformulating the LNP or reconsidering the delivery strategy.

    Clinical Development

    If AETh3R demonstrates acceptable safety and pharmacodynamic activity in preclinical models, its clinical development will be informed by the pathway used for vepdegestrant, the first FDA-approved heterobifunctional protein degrader. More specifically, we plan to employ a combined Phase 1/2 dose-escalation and expansion study, followed by a randomised Phase 3 trial against standard of care [49][50][53]. Because AETh3R combines two new investigational agents, the programme will be designed to show that each agent contributes to the combination's effect, and the FDA will be consulted early on the combination strategy and trial design [51][54].

    Phase 1/2: dose escalation and expansion

    The first-in-human study will be an open-label trial in patients with advanced KRAS-mutant, TP53 wild-type solid tumours whose tumours are αvβ6-positive, using analytically validated biomarker assays [49][55].

    In Part A, the antibody-LNP MDM2 p-PROTAC and the antibody-LNP KRAS p-PROTAC will be tested separately to identify dose-limiting toxicities and recommended Phase 2 doses [49][51]. Because MDM2-targeting agents commonly cause low platelet and neutrophil counts, haematologic toxicity will be monitored closely [56]. In Part B, the two agents will be given together, starting from doses cleared in Part A, to find a tolerable combination dose [49][51]. Infusions will use premedication and step-up dosing to reduce infusion reactions to the lipid carrier, and an independent committee will oversee safety [55][57].

    Paired tumour biopsies before and after treatment will assess target degradation, p53 pathway restoration and MAPK pathway suppression. Blood samples will assess pharmacokinetics and anti-drug antibodies against the peptide cargo and the targeting antibody [49].

    Expansion cohorts in NSCLC will then randomise patients between the MDM2 agent alone, the KRAS agent alone, the combination, and standard of care, with objective response rate as the primary endpoint [53][54]. This design is intended to show whether each agent contributes to the combination's effect [51][54].

    Phase 3: confirmatory efficacy

    Phase 3 will be a randomised, open-label trial comparing the combination with standard-of-care treatment in patients with KRAS-mutant, TP53 wild-type, αvβ6-positive NSCLC who have progressed after platinum chemotherapy and a checkpoint inhibitor [58]. Progression-free survival assessed by blinded independent central review will be a primary endpoint [50][58], with overall survival included in the testing hierarchy, as the FDA regards it as the gold standard in NSCLC [59].

    Continued Evaluation & Patient-Centered Long-Term Tracking

    After potential regulatory approval, long-term safety monitoring and real-world efficacy tracking will be important to ensure that therapeutic benefits translate into a manageable patient experience. Phase IV post-marketing surveillance and non-interventional studies will monitor rare or delayed toxicities in a broader patient population, including potential LNP-related immunogenicity, anti-drug antibody development and rare organ toxicities, addressing concerns regarding unexpected side effects [60]. Concurrently, real-world evidence (RWE) gathered from electronic health records, claims databases and registries, alongside integrated Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), will help bridge the gap between controlled clinical trials and everyday clinical practice [61][62]. These complementary approaches may support evaluation of overall survival, treatment durability, safety and health-related quality of life across diverse patient populations [60][61].

    Finally, to investigate long-term efficacy and potential drug resistance, longitudinal circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) analysis and tumour biopsies, where clinically feasible, may be used to identify emerging resistance mechanisms [63]. These investigations will examine potential changes in E3 ligases or target proteins and their binding sites, alterations in LNP uptake or intracellular delivery, and changes in ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) activity that could reduce target degradation [63][64]. Because these mechanisms have not yet been established for AETh3R, they will be treated as hypotheses for investigation. Findings from resistance profiling and post-marketing safety surveillance could inform the design of second-generation constructs and guide further optimisation of LNP composition, drug stability and targeted delivery. Any resulting modifications would require appropriate preclinical evaluation and subsequent clinical testing. Through this iterative development strategy, AETh3R could be refined to address emerging resistance, improve tolerability and potentially reduce treatment burden while maintaining a focus on patient safety and quality of life.
