    PROTAC

    Most drugs work by occupancy: a molecule sits in an active site and blocks it, and it has to stay there for the effect to last. That approach fails for a large part of the proteome — proteins that drive disease without offering a druggable pocket, so there is nothing for a conventional inhibitor to occupy.

    A PROTAC works differently. Rather than blocking a protein, it destroys it.

    A PROTAC is a bifunctional molecule with three parts: one end binds the protein to be removed, the other recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase — an enzyme the cell already uses to mark proteins for disposal — and a linker holds the two together. When both ends engage at once, the molecule forms a ternary complex that brings the ligase into contact with a protein it would never normally touch.[1] The ligase does what it always does: it tags the protein with ubiquitin, and the proteasome degrades it.

    Why degrade MDM2 rather than inhibit it

    For our target the distinction is not academic. MDM2 is the principal negative regulator of p53, and blocking that interaction to restore p53 activity is a well-established strategy[2] — but MDM2 is itself a p53 target gene. Activating p53 drives transcription of more MDM2, a negative feedback loop that the cell uses to keep p53 in check.[3]

    An occupancy inhibitor therefore fights a moving target: it raises p53, p53 raises MDM2, and the inhibitor must outcompete a growing pool of the very protein it is blocking. Degradation breaks that loop, because the protein is removed rather than occupied. This is the central reason we pursued a degrader rather than another inhibitor.

    An inhibitor must remain bound to keep working, so its effect tracks its concentration and its residence time on the target. A PROTAC is not consumed in the reaction: once the target is ubiquitinated and released to the proteasome, the degrader is free to engage another copy. It acts catalytically rather than stoichiometrically,[4] so a single molecule can drive the destruction of many targets and useful activity is possible at lower concentrations.

    The consequence is that duration of effect is set by how quickly the cell resynthesises the protein, not by how long the drug stays bound. Recovery requires new protein synthesis rather than simple dissociation, which can give a longer-lived effect than occupancy alone would predict.

    Most PROTACs are built from small molecules. Ours is a peptide[5] at both ends, joined by a short flexible peptide linker. The advantages are real: peptides can cover the large, flat interfaces that defeat small molecules, they are designable with protein-design tools rather than medicinal chemistry, and a fully canonical construct is genetically encodable.

    The trade-offs are equally real, and worth stating plainly. Peptides cross membranes poorly and are vulnerable to proteases — which is precisely why the VHL recruiter we adopted carries non-canonical residues that improve its stability, and why delivery is treated as a problem in its own right rather than an afterthought.

    The human genome encodes some 600 E3 ligases[6], but only a handful have well-characterised small-molecule or peptide recruitment sites. VHL is among the best understood: it recognises hydroxyproline through a defined sensing mechanism[7], has a solved structure in complex with its natural substrate, and is widely used in the degrader literature — so a recruiter built on it comes with both a dependable anchor and a benchmark for our own methods.

    One counterintuitive property shapes how degraders must be evaluated. At high concentration a PROTAC can saturate its target and its ligase separately, forming two-component complexes instead of the productive three-component one. Efficacy then falls as concentration rises — the hook effect.[8] It is the clearest demonstration that what matters is not how tightly either end binds, but whether the ternary complex forms. That is why our validation focuses on the assembled construct rather than the binding modules alone, and it is a theme the results return to.

    How we built ours

    Constructing a peptide PROTAC required four things, developed in two independent branches that meet only at the linker. The warhead that binds MDM2 was designed de novo, because we wanted a binder that was genuinely ours rather than one borrowed from the literature. The recruiter that engages VHL was adopted from published work, since a validated anchor gives the assembled degrader a dependable end and a reference point for our methods. A linker then had to be sized and oriented so both ends could engage at the same time, and the assembled molecule validated as a complete ternary complex.

    Inhibitor Design

    Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) remain challenging therapeutic targets because they involve large, relatively flat interfaces poorly suited to conventional small molecules. Peptide inhibitors offer an alternative, reproducing the extended intermolecular contacts required for high-affinity binding while retaining specificity.

    To identify novel MDM2-binding peptides we built an integrated computational pipeline: RFdiffusion for de novo backbone generation, ProteinMPNN for sequence design, AlphaFold Multimer for structure prediction, a three-stage selection funnel, and explicit-solvent molecular dynamics for validation. The selection funnel is the core methodological contribution: it evolved through three successive scripts, each added to correct a blind spot in the previous one, and that evolution is itself an instructive result about how AI-based confidence metrics can mislead peptide design.

    p53–MDM2 Interface Analysis

    Kussie et al. (1996) [9] showed that the N-terminal domain of MDM2 presents a deep hydrophobic groove accommodating an amphipathic α-helix from the p53 transactivation domain (PDB: 1YCR). Three p53 residues, Phe19, Trp23, Leu26, insert into three sub-pockets and form the canonical hydrophobic triad, the minimal pharmacophore for MDM2 inhibition.

    The three sub-pockets were defined directly from 1YCR by interatomic-contact analysis in ChimeraX: each MDM2 residue making a heavy-atom contact (≤ 4.5 Å) with a native anchor was assigned to that anchor’s pocket, with shared wall residues permitted in more than one pocket. This yields the Phe19 pocket (MDM2 residues 58, 61, 62, 67, 72, 75, 93), the Trp23 pocket (54, 57, 58, 61, 91, 93, 99), and the Leu26 pocket (54, 93, 96, 99), in 1YCR numbering. Because the receptor is derived from 1YCR, these pocket definitions make the pocket-occupancy test a direct measure of whether a design binds where p53 binds.

    Literature-Derived MDM2 Inhibitors

    PMI (TSFAEYWNLLSP) was chosen as the primary positive control: a high-affinity peptide ligand (Kd ≈ 3.3 nM [10]) with a crystal structure and the native p53(17–29) peptide (SQETFSDLWKLLP) were included as additional references. Throughout the pipeline, computational predictions for de novo peptides were compared against PMI, which serves as a qualitative benchmark for interface quality and predicted affinity.

    De Novo Inhibitor Design

    Backbones were generated with the RFdiffusion Google Colab notebook [11, 12] using 1YCR as the receptor template, MDM2 fixed, and contig constraints on the interface hotspots (L54, I61, Y67, V93). 500 backbones (50 diffusion iterations each) were generated; ProteinMPNN [12] produced four sequences per backbone, giving 2,000 candidate peptides. Every candidate was predicted in complex with MDM2 by AlphaFold Multimer [13], yielding per-candidate iPTM, pLDDT, PAE, and RMSD. All subsequent structural analysis was performed on these AlphaFold-Multimer structures.

    RFdiffusion is a generative diffusion model for protein structure. It starts from a cloud of random atomic coordinates and, over a series of denoising steps, gradually sculpts them into a physically plausible protein backbone — the same principle as image-generating diffusion models, but operating on 3D protein geometry rather than pixels. It was trained on experimentally solved structures, so it has effectively learned the rules of how real proteins fold.

    For our purpose we held the MDM2 structure fixed and told the model to build new peptide backbones inside the p53-binding groove, anchored to the hotspot residues that line the cleft. The result is a set of 500 candidate backbone shapes geometrically compatible with the target site — the raw scaffolds onto which sequences are later designed. At this stage the peptides have a shape but no amino-acid identity yet.

    All the generated backbones can be found on our GitLab Repository.

    A backbone is only a shape; it still needs an amino-acid sequence that will fold into it. ProteinMPNN is a neural network that solves this "inverse folding" problem: given a 3D backbone, it predicts which sequence of residues is most likely to adopt it, reading the geometry of each position and its neighbours to propose chemically sensible amino acids for the whole chain.

    We ran it on each of the 500 backbones and asked for four sequences per backbone, turning 500 scaffolds into 2,000 candidate peptides. Multiple sequences per backbone add diversity and let us later see which backbones are robust — several of their sequences succeed rather than a single lucky variant.

    All the generated peptides can be found on our GitLab Repository.

    Having a designed sequence is not proof it will bind. AlphaFold Multimer predicts the 3D structure of a protein complex from the sequences of its components — here each designed peptide paired with MDM2 — effectively asking what structure the two chains would form and how confident that prediction is.

    For every one of the 2,000 candidates it returns the predicted complex plus confidence scores (iPTM, pLDDT, PAE, RMSD), which the first selection stage ranks on. Crucially, high confidence means the model is sure of the predicted shape, not that the peptide truly binds — the limitation the later stages were built to address.

    The final list of metrics generated by the notebook can be found in our GitLab Repository.

    Candidate Selection

    Selection evolved through three scripts, each correcting a limitation of the last. The three peptides carried through validation each emerged from a different stage, and together they trace this evolution.

    Stage 1 — Composite Z-score (confidence ranking).

    The first script ranked candidates by a composite Z-score of the AlphaFold confidence metrics. Each metric was standardized against the distribution of that metric across all 2,000 candidates,

    Z = (x − μ) / σ

    and the standardized values were combined into a single score,

    Zcomposite = ZiPTM + ZpLDDT − ZPAE − ZRMSD

    so that designs with high interface confidence, high per-residue confidence, low predicted error, and low geometric deviation score highest. This reproduces the ranking logic of the ColabDesign pipeline's own output.

    The top-ranked design was 42.3 (SVEENMKEFEKMILG; “Z1”) — iPTM 0.828, pLDDT 0.913, PAE 3.73, RMSD 0.41. It was carried forward as the confidence-optimal candidate.

    Stage 2 — Filtered ranking with pharmacophore flags.Because a raw composite mixes confident designs with marginal ones, the second script applied a confidence filter first (iPTM ≥ 0.70, PAE ≤ 10, RMSD ≤ 2.0, pLDDT ≥ 0.80), then ranked the survivors and flagged the p53-style helical anchor register and Trp presence. Of the 2,000 candidates, 189 sequences across 85 backbones passed the filter.

    From this stage we selected 267.3 (SEIEELWEELIEEAE; "Z2") — iPTM 0.847, pLDDT 0.922, PAE 3.64, RMSD 1.02 — as the highest-ranked design carrying a Trp and the anchor register, i.e. the best apparent p53 mimic. It ranked third overall by the filtered composite.

    Stage 3 — Chemistry and interface ranking. Inspection of 267.3 exposed the flaw that motivated the third script. Despite its high confidence and its "mimic" flags, 267.3 carries a strongly negative net charge, a glutamate-rich sequence that cannot plausibly bind the hydrophobic MDM2 groove. This was not an isolated case: across the pool, strongly acidic sequences were distributed throughout the entire composite-Z range rather than penalized, because AlphaFold confidence metrics score predicted geometry and are blind to interface chemistry.

    The third script therefore scored the predicted complexes directly on interface geometry and chemistry, computed from the atomic coordinates: apolar buried surface area per residue (FreeSASA [14]); occupancy of the three sub-pockets; intermolecular contact count (≤ 5 Å [15, 16]); a chemistry gate on net charge; and a confidence gate on interface pLDDT. Candidates were selected by an intersection rule, passing all gates, occupying all three pockets, and meeting or exceeding the PMI benchmark (40.0 apolar Å²/res, 35 contacts, 3/3 pockets) on both apolar BSA per residue and contact count.

    The third script judges a design by looking directly at the predicted contact between peptide and MDM2 rather than at the model's confidence. Buried Surface Area (BSA) is how much of the peptide's surface becomes hidden from solvent on docking; a more buried hydrophobic ("apolar") surface means a tighter, more water-excluding fit, which drives binding in this pocket. Sub-pocket occupancy checks whether the peptide reaches into all three hydrophobic sub-pockets the natural p53 anchors fill. Contact count is how many residue-to-residue contacts the interface makes. Two gates then act as pass/fail filters: the chemistry gate rejects peptides whose net charge is far outside the range of real MDM2 binders (this catches the glutamate-rich 267.3), and the confidence gate drops predictions the model itself was unsure about. Only designs clearing both gates, filling all three pockets, and matching or beating PMI on buried surface and contacts are selected — an "all of the above" rule rather than a single score, so no one weak spot can be hidden by a strong one.

    Applied to the 2,000 candidates, 1,275 occupied all three sub-pockets, 409 passed all gates, and 18 designs (from 11 backbones) met or exceeded PMI. Z2 was rejected at the chemistry gate, confirming the script did what it was built to do. The top-ranked design, and the most reproducible backbone in the pool (all four of its ProteinMPNN sequences pass), was 131.2 (MKPEEVGEMLLSVLK; "Z3").

    Validation

    The candidates were characterised by an independent structure predictor (AlphaFold3), binding-affinity estimation (PRODIGY), and explicit-solvent molecular dynamics.

    Cross-predictor comparison (AlphaFold3). Because the pipeline runs on AlphaFold Multimer, the candidates were re-predicted with AlphaFold3 [17] to test predictor dependence. Both predictors place all candidates in the p53 cleft occupying all three sub-pockets. They disagree on interface tightness: AF3 packs the designs it assigns low confidence (notably Z3, domain iPTM 0.55) more loosely than Multimer does. The disagreement is systematic, so absolute interface metrics are predictor-dependent, and molecular dynamics was used as the decisive test.

    Binding affinity (PRODIGY). Affinity was estimated from AlphaFold3 structures with PRODIGY [15]. On the full-length receptor, PMI is estimated at 16 nM (measured 3.3 nM), Z1 at 99 nM, Z2 at 96 nM, and Z3 at 0.99 μM. The estimates are treated as a relative ranking calibrated to PMI, not as absolute constants.

    Molecular dynamics. Each candidate–MDM2 complex was simulated from its AlphaFold-Multimer structure — consistent with selection, for 100 ns in GROMACS 2022 [18] (AMBER99SB-ILDN [19], TIP3P [20], 0.15 M NaCl, 300 K, 1 bar), analysed with GROMACS tools and MDAnalysis [21]. Systems were placed in a cubic box with a minimum 1.0 nm solute-to-edge separation, solvated, neutralised, energy-minimised, equilibrated under NVT then NPT with position restraints on protein heavy atoms, and released for unrestrained production. As a computational cross-check, interface SASA and BSA from FreeSASA were validated against ChimeraX on the PMI and 131.2 structures: total complex SASA agreed to within 0.1% and interface BSA to within ~10%, consistent with expected probe-radius differences and confirming the interface metrics are implementation-independent.

    After least-squares superposition onto a reference frame, the RMSD is the root-mean-square distance between corresponding backbone atoms in the current and reference structures — a single number per frame describing how far the structure has drifted. A rise followed by a maintained plateau indicates relaxation to a nearby equilibrium and is the signature of a stable complex; a curve that keeps climbing indicates progressive dissociation or unfolding.

    Rg measures the compactness of the complex — the mass-weighted spread of atoms about the centre of mass. A flat, tightly distributed Rg means the assembly keeps constant overall dimensions (neither swelling nor compacting), the hallmark of an intact folded complex; a systematic upward drift would indicate unfolding or partial dissociation.

    SASA is the surface area the solvent can reach. At an interface, a low and steady SASA means buried residues stay buried; a rising trace would mean interface residues are becoming re-exposed to water — dissociation in progress. It reports directly on whether the binding surface remains sequestered.

    All five complexes remain bound over 100 ns. No trajectory shows dissociation or unfolding: every backbone RMSD reaches a plateau rather than drifting upward, every radius of gyration stays within a narrow band, and no solvent-accessible surface area rises progressively. The metrics therefore separate the peptides by degree of tightness.

    Verdict

    The three-stage funnel yields a coherent conclusion, and each candidate earns its place for a different reason. Z1 (42.3) is the design AlphaFold confidence ranks highest, and it is a valid, persistently bound complex, but it does not reach the PMI interface benchmark, and it was the most backbone-mobile candidate in simulation. High predicted confidence did not translate into either the best interface or the tightest dynamics.

    Z2 (267.3) exposed the central weakness of confidence-based selection: a high-scoring, apparent p53 mimic that is chemically incompatible with the target (net charge −8). It is retained precisely as the example that justified adding interface and chemistry scoring and, instructively, it is among the better-behaved designs in molecular dynamics, demonstrating that dynamic stability alone cannot rescue a chemically implausible binder. Z3 (131.2) is the candidate produced once selection accounts for interface geometry and chemistry: the highest interface burial in the pool (42.0 apolar Å²/res), the most reproducible backbone — all four of its ProteinMPNN sequences clear the benchmark and chemically compatible with the groove at net charge −1.

    On the combined evidence, Z3 is the leading MDM2-binding candidate, being the only one of the three to meet the PMI interface benchmark on both buried surface and contacts while occupying all three native sub-pockets. That conclusion rests on interface quality and backbone reproducibility rather than on dynamics. It should be stated plainly that all evidence here is computational: the pipeline reduces the number of candidates requiring experimental testing and increases confidence in those selected; it does not replace experimental validation.

    Literature-Derived VHL Binder

    The de novo pipeline described above furnishes an MDM2-binding warhead: a peptide that occupies the p53-binding cleft and is supported by structure prediction, affinity estimation, and dynamic stability. A PROTAC, however, is bifunctional. Alongside the moiety that engages the protein of interest, it must carry a second ligand that recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase, so that the target is drawn into productive proximity with the ubiquitination machinery and marked for proteasomal degradation. The MDM2 binder therefore constitutes only one terminus of the intended peptide-PROTAC (p-PROTAC); the complementary terminus requires a validated E3-ligase recruiter.

    Von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) was selected as the ligase to be hijacked. VHL is the substrate-recognition subunit of the CRL2VHL Cullin-RING complex and one of the most extensively exploited E3 ligases in targeted protein degradation, with a well-defined, structurally characterised recruitment site. As with the MDM2 side of the workflow, rather than design a recruiter de novo we adopted an experimentally validated peptide from the literature. A known binder provides a dependable anchor for the assembled degrader and, as with the PMI control, a benchmark against which the surrounding computational protocol can be calibrated.

    The pHIF1 recruiter

    Under normoxia, VHL recognises 4-hydroxyproline (Hyp) generated post-translationally on the oxygen-dependent degradation domain of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α); the hydroxyproline hydroxyl is the single most important determinant of the HIF–VHL interaction. The recruiter used here is the modified HIF-1α–derived hexapeptide LA-Hyp-Y-Hle-P, hereafter pHIF1, taken from the stapled-peptide PROTAC work of Chen et al. [22].

    Two non-canonical substitutions distinguish pHIF1 from the minimal native motif. First, 4-hydroxyproline replaces the common proline: because the Hyp hydroxyl is critical for VHL engagement, presenting it correctly is essential to binding. Second, homoleucine (Hle), which carries a longer side chain than the native isoleucine, occupies the hydrophobic cavity of VHL; structural modelling indicated that this pocket can accommodate the extended side chain. The two modifications were reported not only to strengthen the HIF–VHL interaction but also to enhance the proteolytic resistance of the peptide, improving affinity and stability simultaneously.

    The presence of two residues outside the standard amino-acid alphabet is not a cosmetic detail; it dictates every subsequent stage of the evaluation. Sequence-based structure predictors and standard protein force fields both assume canonical residues, so pHIF1 cannot be treated as an ordinary peptide chain.     Structural validation with Boltz-2

    AlphaFold Multimer and AlphaFold3, as deployed for the MDM2 candidates, operate on canonical sequences and cannot fold a chain containing non-standard residues. The VHL–pHIF1 complex was therefore predicted with Boltz-2 [23], an open, AlphaFold3-class biomolecular foundation model, run through the Neurosnap server. Boltz-2 accepts explicit ligand chemistry and can consequently model a complex in which the recruiter carries Hyp and Hle, which a purely sequence-based predictor cannot. Agreement between a distinct predictor and the established crystallographic binding mode again provides stronger evidence for the pose than confidence from any single method.

    The VHL receptor was supplied as the sequence used in the reference crystal structure of the VHL–HIF-1α complex (PDB: 1LM8) [7], the structure that originally established the hydroxyproline-recognition mode being exploited here. The predicted complexes were assessed using the interface confidence metrics returned by the server, the interface predicted TM-score (ipTM), per-residue pLDDT, and the predicted aligned error across the interface, and inspected visually to confirm that pHIF1 seated its hydroxyproline into the recognition pocket of VHL rather than settling at an incidental surface site. This verification is not redundant with the confidence scores: as with the MDM2 groove, a model may be predicted confidently while placing the peptide away from the intended site.

    Binding-affinity estimation with PRODIGY

    The highest-confidence predicted complex was submitted to PRODIGY [15] for binding-affinity estimation. Because pHIF1 was treated as a small-molecule ligand rather than a polypeptide, the protein–ligand formulation of the method was used, predicting affinity from the number and chemical character of the intermolecular contacts. The estimated binding free energy was ΔG = −8.75 kcal mol⁻¹, corresponding to a dissociation constant of Kd ≈ 0.4 μM.

    As stressed for the de novo MDM2 candidates, absolute PRODIGY values for interfaces of this size carry substantial uncertainty and are most reliable as a relative ranking rather than as absolute constants. The sub-micromolar estimate is nonetheless in the range expected for an experimentally validated VHL recruiter and gives no reason to reject pHIF1 before the more demanding dynamic test.

    Molecular-dynamics simulations

    Whether the predicted VHL–pHIF1 pose persists under physiological conditions was assessed by explicit-solvent molecular dynamics, following the same GROMACS 2022 [18] protocol used for the MDM2 complexes on the Aristotle HPC cluster, with the modifications required by the non-canonical peptide.

    Because Hyp and Hle lie outside the AMBER99SB-ILDN [19] alphabet, the peptide was treated as a small-molecule ligand. Force-field parameters for pHIF1 were generated with the General AMBER Force Field, version 2 (GAFF2) [24] via ACPYPE [25], following the protein–ligand preparation protocol of Lemkul [26]. The VHL protein was parametrised with AMBER99SB-ILDN as before, and the two topologies were combined prior to solvation. The starting conformation was the Boltz-2 prediction of the complex. Systems were solvated with TIP3P water [20] in a cubic box with a minimum 1.0 nm solute-to-edge separation, neutralised with chloride counter-ions, energy-minimised, equilibrated under NVT and then NPT with position restraints on both the protein backbone and the peptide ligand, and released for production dynamics. Trajectories were analysed with native GROMACS tools and MDAnalysis [21].

    Interpretation and outlook

    The three validation stages now converge for the VHL–pHIF1 complex, as they did for the MDM2 candidates. Boltz-2 places the modified hydroxyproline in its recognition pocket; PRODIGY returns a sub-micromolar affinity consistent with a validated recruiter; and, over the full 150 ns trajectory, the molecular dynamics resolve into an unambiguous, maintained RMSD plateau (settling to ~0.33 nm), a constant radius of gyration (~1.83 nm), a stable SASA (~105 nm²), and an RMSF profile that holds the interface residues rigid while confining motion to the chain termini. On the evidence of all three orthogonal tests, pHIF1 is a well-supported VHL-recruiting module and an appropriate E3-ligase terminus for the intended degrader.

    With an MDM2-binding warhead and a VHL-recruiting pHIF1 module both computationally supported, the remaining step is to join them through a linker to form the bifunctional p-PROTAC, and then to evaluate and iterate on the assembled construct, in particular the geometry and cooperativity of the ternary MDM2·p-PROTAC·VHL complex, on which productive ubiquitin transfer ultimately depends.

    As with every result in this workflow, the evidence here is computational. The pipeline is designed to reduce the number of candidates requiring experimental testing and to increase confidence in those selected, not to replace experimental validation. Confirmation of VHL binding requires biophysical measurement, and confirmation of degradation requires cellular assay.

    Linker Design

    A peptide PROTAC has three parts: a moiety that binds the target protein, a moiety that recruits an E3 ligase, and a linker joining them. We designed the two binding peptides separately, so the linker was the final element needed to complete each bifunctional molecule.

    The linker sets the distance and relative orientation of the two proteins in the ternary complex, so its length is a constrained parameter rather than a free one. An insufficient length prevents complex formation, whereas an excessive length introduces conformational flexibility and an entropic penalty to ternary-complex assembly and stability.

    We therefore approached linker design in two stages: first, determining the distance between the two attachment points when both peptides are bound; second, converting that distance into a defined linker sequence.

    Positioning the Two Binders

    The target protein and the E3 ligase have no natural interface. In a PROTAC, they are brought together only because the linker ties the two binding peptides into one molecule. This means there is no biologically correct pose to find through shape complementarity, the geometry is set by the linker, not by the proteins. Our docking step therefore asks a focused question: in which arrangements can the two peptide attachment points be brought close enough to be joined?

    To answer this, we docked the two binary complexes, the target protein with its bound peptide, and VHL with its bound peptide, against each other, and measured the distance between the two atoms the linker would connect.

    We built each binding peptide against its own protein and combined them only at the docking stage. This follows established PROTAC modelling protocols, which dock a target–ligand complex against an E3–ligand complex rather than predicting the whole assembly at once [27, 28]. Predicting a non-native, four-part assembly directly is unreliable, because structure-prediction methods depend on evolutionary signal that does not exist for an artificial complex.

    Setting a Distance Ceiling

    Before docking, we defined an upper bound on the terminus-to-terminus distance. This is a screening cutoff, not a prediction: its only role is to exclude arrangements where the two attachment points end up too far apart for any plausible linker to bridge.

    We set the ceiling at 30 Å, and anchored this value in the empirical behaviour of characterised degraders rather than choosing a round number. Across published PROTACs, effective linkers span a wide range — from as few as ~3 atoms to ~29 atoms — with a system-specific optimum in each case [29]. VHL-based systems, which are directly relevant to our design, show the same pattern. In a series of VHL-recruiting TBK1 degraders, linkers ranged from 7 to 29 atoms: no degradation was observed below ~12 atoms, while all lengths between 12 and 29 atoms produced sub-micromolar degradation, with only a modest decline in potency at the longest end [29].

    Translating this upper range into peptide residues gives our ceiling. An extended chain of ~29 atoms corresponds to roughly 30 Å, which at ~3.5–3.8 Å per residue equals about eight residues [30, 31]. An eight-residue ceiling therefore sits at the generous top of what has produced working degraders — a conservative, non-excluding cap that discards only the geometrically implausible while retaining every arrangement a realistic linker could reach.

    The final linker length comes from the measured distance in the retained poses, not from the ceiling. As long as the bound is generous enough not to discard valid arrangements, its exact value does not affect the result. This matches the loose-constraint strategy used in published PROTAC docking, where an over-tight bound risks discarding the true geometry, while a permissive one only admits poses that later filtering removes [28].

    Restraint-Guided Docking

    We docked each pair of binary complexes in HADDOCK [32] using an ab-initio protocol with centre-of-mass restraints, so the two proteins stayed in contact without being forced into a defined interface. The 30 Å ceiling was applied as an unambiguous distance restraint between the two linkage atoms.

    The restraint is a soft energy penalty, not a hard wall. It is a flat-bottomed potential: no penalty below 30 Å, and a rising penalty beyond it. This lets the docking exceed the bound where other energetic terms strongly favour a wider arrangement — which is itself informative, as we describe below.

    Evaluating the Docked Poses

    We assessed the resulting clusters on two criteria.

    Distance. For each cluster, we measured the gap between the two linkage atoms in ChimeraX. We report the range across clusters rather than a single value, because the arrangement is not unique. Gaps well below 30 Å indicate that the ceiling was non-binding and the result is robust to its exact value. Gaps clustering at or beyond 30 Å indicate a binding ceiling, evidence that the two attachment points can only be bridged by a near-fully-extended linker, and so a marker of a poor orientation rather than a value to tune.

    Orientation. We inspected each pose to confirm that the two termini face each other across an open gap, rather than the measured distance passing through a protein core. Because the two proteins have no real interface, some clusters are geometrically implausible despite reasonable scores, and these were discarded.

    Choosing the Linkage Orientation

    Each PROTAC can be assembled in two directions, which give different geometries. We define them as:

    • Forward orientation.The MDM2 inhibitor C-terminus joined to the VHL-binder N-terminus.
    • Inverse orientation.The VHL-binder C-terminus joined to the MDM2 inhibitor N-terminus.

    We docked and measured both orientations for each binder, and chose the one giving the shorter, more favourable distance distribution. The optimal orientation is binder-specific: it depends on the exit vectors of each peptide’s termini and cannot be assumed in advance.

    For our binders, 42.3 favoured the forward orientation, while PMI, 267.3 and 131.2 favoured the inverted orientation. The optimal direction is therefore not a property of the target or the recruiter but of the individual warhead: three of the four constructs place the VHL recruiter at the N-terminal end, while 42.3 is joined in the opposite sense. This asymmetry is carried through into the assembled constructs and, as the ternary simulations show, has consequences for how the two proteins are positioned relative to one another.

    Converting Distance to Linker Length

    With the optimal orientation and its distance range fixed, we converted the measured gap into a residue count.

    An extended peptide backbone spans ~3.5 Å per residue, but a flexible glycine–serine linker behaves as a random coil, so its typical end-to-end distance is much shorter than its fully extended length [31]. Building a linker to exactly the measured distance would leave it rigid and strained, unable to let the ternary complex settle. We therefore set the contour length to roughly 1.5–2× the measured gap, giving conformational slack.

    For representative gaps of ~11–16 Å, this gives linkers of about five to nine residues. All four binders, each in its optimal orientation, converged on similar linker lengths in this range.

    Linker Sequence and Final Constructs

    We used flexible glycine–serine (GS) linkers. Their properties suit the requirements of a peptide PROTAC: glycine provides backbone flexibility, allowing the two peptides to sample the relative orientations needed to form the ternary complex, while serine contributes polarity and aqueous solubility. GS repeats are among the most widely used flexible linkers in peptide PROTAC design for exactly these reasons [5, 30].

    Linker composition is as important as length. A flexible GS linker gives the two binding moieties the conformational freedom to engage both proteins simultaneously, whereas a rigid or hydrophobic linker can restrict productive arrangements or reduce solubility [5]. Because our binders each require only a short spacer (approximately five to nine residues), a simple GS repeat provides adequate reach and flexibility without adding unnecessary bulk.

    Our docking narrows the length to a defensible range; it does not identify a single optimum. Linker length is a decisive factor in PROTAC activity, and the best value often cannot be predicted from geometry alone, because it also depends on ternary-complex cooperativity that distance measurements do not capture [5]. The computed length is therefore intended as the centre of a short synthetic series to be tested experimentally.

    Limitations

    Distance is necessary but not sufficient. Our pipeline confirms that a linker of a given length can span the two attachment points; it does not predict how efficiently the resulting PROTAC will degrade its target. Productive degradation also depends on the induced protein–protein interface and on the geometry of ubiquitin transfer, neither of which is captured by a distance measurement.

    For docking, we made one substitution in the VHL binder. The binder contains homoleucine (Hle), a non-canonical residue that is not in HADDOCK’s residue library and so cannot be processed directly. We replaced it with leucine, which differs from homoleucine by a single methylene in the side chain and has an identical backbone. Because the docking measures only a terminus-to-terminus distance, set by backbone geometry and the positions of the terminal atoms, not by side-chain length, this change does not affect the measured value. The homoleucine is retained in the final synthesised construct.

    PROTAC Assembly and Validation

    Assembled constructs

    Each bifunctional degrader joins a validated MDM2-binding warhead to the pHIF1 VHL recruiter through a flexible glycine–serine linker, assembled in the orientation determined by the docking analysis above. Four constructs were built: a PMI-based reference degrader and three carrying the de novo warheads.

    The orientation follows from the exit vectors of each peptide’s termini. PZ1 joins its warhead C-terminus to the recruiter N-terminus (forward), while PMI-PROTAC, PZ2 and PZ3 join the recruiter C-terminus to the warhead N-terminus (inverted). Linker lengths of six and eight residues sit within the five-to-nine-residue range indicated by the measured docking distances, providing conformational slack without unnecessary bulk.

    Each construct exists in two forms. The synthesised molecule carries the non-canonical residues that give pHIF1 its affinity and protease resistance: 4-hydroxyproline (Hyp) and homoleucine (Hle). Hle was replaced leucine wherever a tool could not process modified residues. The substitution is conservative, Hle differs from leucine by a single methylene, and both share an identical backbone, so backbone geometry and terminus positions are unaffected.

    Building the ternary complexes

    The input structures for simulation were the assembled PROTACs bound simultaneously to MDM2 and to the VHL–ElonginB/ElonginC (VBC) complex, predicted with AlphaFold3. Two practical constraints shaped this step. AlphaFold3 supports Hyp as a post-translational modification, so hydroxyproline was modelled directly in place of the corresponding proline; homoleucine has no equivalent representation and was therefore modelled as leucine. Boltz-2, which handled the non-canonical VHL recruiter in isolation, could not be used here because the assembled PROTACs exceed the size it accepts as a ligand.

    Molecular dynamics followed the same protocol as the de novo inhibitor simulations — GROMACS 2022 with AMBER99SB-ILDN and TIP3P water, 0.15 M NaCl, 300 K and 1 bar, and 100 ns of unrestrained production after staged equilibration. No special ligand parametrisation was required, because hydroxyproline is present in the force field as a recognised heteroatom residue.

    Nine analyses were extracted from the trajectories, covering the three interfaces of the assembly: each PROTAC against MDM2, each PROTAC against VBC, and the induced protein–protein interface between MDM2 and VHL, together with the solvent exposure of each component and of the complete complex.

    All four ternary complexes are stable over 100 ns. No minimum distance drifts upward, no contact count collapses, and no solvent-accessible surface area rises progressively; every complex compacts over roughly the first 20–25 ns and then holds its arrangement. The first and most important conclusion is therefore a negative one: attaching a linker and an E3-recruiting module does not disrupt warhead engagement of MDM2, and the assembled degraders sustain simultaneous contact with both proteins.

    Warhead engagement is retained in the rank order predicted for the isolated peptides. At the MDM2 interface, PZ3 makes the most contacts (~800–1000) and PZ1 the fewest (~500–600), with PMI-PROTAC and PZ2 between them. That PZ3 leads here is consistent with 131.2 having been selected for the highest interface burial in the candidate pool: its favourable binding geometry survives incorporation into the full bifunctional construct.

    The induced MDM2–VHL interface tells a different and more consequential story. PZ1 forms by far the most extensive protein–protein interface, approximately 450–600 contacts against 300–380 for PMI-PROTAC, 200–300 for PZ2 and 130–200 for PZ3, and holds the two proteins at a notably tight and stable minimum distance of ~0.13–0.14 nm, some 0.05 nm closer than any other construct. PZ1 also engages VBC most extensively. The ordering at this interface is close to the inverse of the ordering at MDM2.

    This trade-off is the central result of the section. PZ3 binds its target best but forms the weakest ternary complex; PZ1 binds its target least well but assembles the most substantial induced interface. Because degradation efficiency depends on ternary-complex formation and cooperativity rather than on target affinity alone, the construct with the strongest warhead is not necessarily the strongest degrader. On the evidence of the induced interface, PZ1 is the most promising degrader candidate — a conclusion the warhead-stage pipeline could not have reached, since 42.3 ranked lowest of the three designs on interface burial and was the most mobile in the isolated-peptide simulations.

    Interpretation and outlook

    The ternary simulations reposition the candidate ranking. Warhead quality and ternary-complex formation are separable properties, and the selection pipeline optimised only the first. PZ1 emerges as the leading degrader candidate on ternary-interface extent and stability; PZ3 retains the strongest target engagement and remains the better candidate if target binding alone is the criterion; PMI-PROTAC behaves as a well-balanced reference, consistent with its role as a validated benchmark.

    Two directions follow. First, because linker length and orientation plausibly drive the differences observed, a short series varying both around the PZ1 geometry would test whether the induced interface can be improved further — the synthetic series the linker analysis anticipated. Second, the off-target behaviour identified for 131.2 against MDMX is being exploited rather than discarded: an MDMX-directed PROTAC built on the Z3 warhead is under construction for simulation, turning an apparent selectivity liability into a deliberate second target.

    As throughout this work, the evidence is computational. These simulations establish that the assembled degraders form stable ternary complexes and rank them by the extent of the induced interface; they do not demonstrate ubiquitin transfer or degradation. Confirmation requires biophysical measurement of ternary-complex formation and cellular degradation assay.

    Extending the Pipeline: KRAS–SOS1

    Having built a design pipeline for one protein–protein interaction, we wanted to know whether it transfers. We chose KRAS as a second target, and the result — a negative one — is what convinced us the pipeline is worth applying there.

    KRAS is among the most consequential oncogenes in human cancer and, for decades, among the least druggable. Its surface is smooth and largely featureless, offering conventional small molecules little to bind. One of the few well-characterised handles is its interaction with SOS1, the guanine nucleotide exchange factor that reloads KRAS with GTP and returns it to its active state. Blocking that interaction prevents reactivation, and because the interface is a defined α-helix inserting into a groove, it is the kind of target our peptide pipeline was built for.

    A literature-derived starting point

    Following the same logic we used for MDM2 — begin with a validated binder before designing a new one — we turned to the work of Leshchiner et al. [33], who reported stabilised α-helices of SOS1 (SAH-SOS1) that engage wild-type and mutant KRAS with nanomolar affinity and disrupt the SOS1–KRAS interaction. Their peptides are modelled on the SOS1 α-helix spanning residues 929–944, identified from the KRAS–SOS1 crystal structure (PDB: 1NVU) [33].

    These peptides are hydrocarbon-stapled: a synthetic crosslink is installed between residues at i and i+4 positions, covalently locking the peptide into its helical conformation. We took the α-helix sequence without the staple, to test whether the bare helix retains useful binding on its own.

    A short peptide excised from a protein usually loses the fold it had in context: with no surrounding structure to hold it, an isolated α-helix unravels into a disordered chain in solution, and an unfolded peptide pays a large entropic penalty to re-fold on binding. A hydrocarbon staple is a covalent brace between two side chains one helical turn apart that pre-organises the peptide into the helix it needs to adopt, restoring affinity and adding protease resistance. We omitted it deliberately: staples require specialised non-canonical amino acids and synthetic chemistry, so a peptide that worked without one would be markedly simpler to produce and, unlike a stapled peptide, genetically encodable.

    Docking the SOS1 helix to KRAS

    We predicted the KRAS–SOS1 helix complex with AlphaFold3 and simulated it under the same explicit-solvent molecular-dynamics protocol used throughout this work, so the results would be directly comparable to our MDM2 systems.

    The prediction returned ipTM = 0.27 against pTM = 0.65. The contrast between the two is the informative part: pTM reflects the model as a whole and is carried by KRAS, which folds confidently on its own, while ipTM reports specifically on the relative placement of the two chains. For comparison, the same predictor assigned our MDM2 designs interface confidences of 0.55–0.86 on the isolated domain and 0.95–0.96 on the full-length receptor. At 0.27 the KRAS–SOS1 helix sits far below even the weakest of those. AlphaFold3 is confident about KRAS and essentially unconvinced that the helix binds it.

    1 / 4

    The two independent lines of evidence therefore point the same way. The unstapled SOS1 helix does not form a stable complex with KRAS — which is not a failure of the peptide so much as confirmation of why the original authors stapled it. Stripped of the crosslink that holds it helical, the peptide has nothing maintaining the conformation KRAS recognises, and both the predictor and the physics register that.

    The conclusion is sharply defined, and it is the reason we consider KRAS a target worth designing for rather than borrowing from: a native interface helix, taken out of context and left unconstrained, is not a viable binder on its own.

    Pathways

    Our therapeutic strategy targets two proteins for degradation: MDM2, the E3 ligase that holds p53 in check, and KRAS, the driver mutated in roughly a third of lung adenocarcinomas. The biological question is not whether either target is druggable but whether degrading both together kills a cancer cell that survives either one alone. That is a question about a network, not about a molecule, and it cannot be answered by inspecting a pathway diagram.

    A549 was chosen as the model system because its genotype makes the question sharp. The line is TP53 wild-type, so the p53 axis is intact and MDM2 degradation should have somewhere to act. It carries KRAS G12S, so RAS signalling is constitutive. CDKN2A is deleted, which removes p14ARF, the endogenous MDM2 inhibitor, making MDM2 the relevant pharmacological lever rather than a redundant one.

    We built a Boolean model. Each protein is a node that is either active or inactive; each node's state is a logical function of the nodes that regulate it. The model makes no claim about concentrations or rate constants — it asks only which stable configurations the network can reach, and how those change when a node is forced off. That abstraction is appropriate here because the readout of interest is itself close to binary: a cell either commits to apoptosis or it does not.

    Network Construction

    The network was assembled from OmniPath, a meta-database that aggregates signed, directed molecular interactions from dozens of curated primary resources [34, 35]. The node list is declared, from the literature, in a versioned text file; OmniPath then supplies the edges among those nodes, each carrying its source resources, PubMed identifiers and curation effort. Nodes outside the declared scope are not silently excluded but ranked in a suggestions file, weighted by how specifically they connect to the model rather than by raw degree, so that promiscuous transcription factors do not crowd out genuine candidates.

    The division matters. Which proteins belong in a model of apoptotic commitment is a scientific judgement that a database cannot make; which interactions exist between them, and on what evidence, is exactly what a database is for.

    The declared scope spans thirty-one proteins across five modules: the MAPK arm (KRAS, RAF1, MAP2K1, MAPK1, MAPK3), the PI3K/AKT arm (PIK3CA, PIK3R1, PTEN, AKT1, FOXO3, GSK3B), the p53 module (TP53, MDM2, MDM4, CDKN1A), the BCL-2 family (BBC3, PMAIP1, BCL2L11, BAD, MCL1, BCL2L1, BAX, BAK1), and the execution machinery (CYCS, DIABLO, APAF1, CASP9, CASP3, CASP7, XIAP, PARP1).

    An aggregated interaction database contains everything from interactions reported in forty papers to those reported once. We required each retained interaction to be recorded as directed, supported by at least two independent resources and one reference, and to reach a curation effort of at least five. Every rejected edge is written to an exclusion file with its reason, and the entire network was rebuilt with all filters relaxed to record which edges the thresholds removed — mostly reverse records of interactions already present.

    One edge was restored by hand against the thresholds. KRAS→PI3K is supported by a single resource and was therefore excluded, which left the whole PI3K/AKT arm disconnected from the perturbed node. Without it, degrading KRAS could not reach AKT, BAD, FOXO3 or GSK3B, and the model could not address the question it was built for. The edge is flagged in the network as a manual addition rather than folded in silently.

    Raw API responses are archived with their URL, timestamp and checksum. OmniPath is updated continuously, so a network is only reproducible against a pinned snapshot. All the network-construction scripts, the declared node list and the archived OmniPath snapshot can be found in our GitLab Repository.

    Curation

    The filtered network contained 117 edges, of which 38 arrived either unsigned or with resources disagreeing about the sign. These were adjudicated by hand, each decision recorded in a table with its reasoning and its reference basis. Twenty-five entries resulted: five sign assignments and twenty removals. A second inspection during rule construction found a further sixteen problems, which are logged in a separate changelog.

    What made the adjudication tractable was that the errors fall into recognisable classes rather than being one-off judgement calls.

    Reverse records. Protein-protein interaction databases record binding without a direction of effect. An edge whose evidence is drawn entirely from such resources (HPRD, BioGRID, IntAct, HINT, DIP) is a co-immunoprecipitation record, and it frequently appears in the reverse orientation of a real signed edge. BAD→AKT1 is the reverse of AKT1 phosphorylating BAD; RAF1→KRAS the reverse of KRAS binding RAF1; MAP2K1→RAF1 the reverse of RAF1 phosphorylating MEK.

    Signs contradicting their own annotations. MAPK1→RAF1 arrived with a consensus sign of activation. The same row listed the modified residues: S259, S289, S296, S301 — the inhibitory feedback cluster. The sign and the mechanism in a single record disagreed, and the sign was wrong.

    Reciprocal sequestration. BH3-only proteins and their guards are recorded from both sides: DIABLO inhibits XIAP and XIAP inhibits DIABLO; BCL2L11 inhibits BCL2L1 and the reverse. Both are true as statements about binding, but in a logical model a mutual inhibition creates a toggle that can latch into either state. We retained the antagonist→guard direction throughout and recorded the reasoning.

    Text-mining artefacts. Mechanism annotations were joined from OmniPath's enzyme-substrate dataset. Several records attributed p53's own phosphorylation sites to p53 as the enzyme — TP53 phosphorylating CDKN1A at S15, for instance, a residue that belongs to p53 itself. All such records traced to automated literature readers (ProtMapper, Sparser, RLIMS-P, REACH), which invert enzyme and substrate when a sentence is written in the passive voice. Records resting solely on these resources are excluded; the curated ubiquitination record for MDM2→TP53 at K370 survives untouched.

    Transcriptional bypasses. p53 is recorded as a transcriptional activator of CASP3, CASP7 and CASP9. Retaining those edges lets p53 fire the executioner caspases without any mitochondrial involvement, which collapses precisely the decision the model exists to represent.

    Two structural changes followed. PIK3CA and PIK3R1 were merged into a single PI3K node, because their recorded mutual activation locks permanently on once either fires. PARP1 was removed: with its cleavage edge gone, its only remaining inputs were transcriptional, so it could not function as an apoptosis readout.

    The corrected network holds 29 nodes and 99 edges.

    All the adjudicated decisions and the full network changelog can be found in our GitLab Repository.

    Rule construction

    An edge list is not a model. It records that KRAS activates RAF1 and that AKT1 inhibits BAD, but it cannot say whether two activators of the same node combine as either or as both. That distinction is the model, and it has to be written down rather than inferred.

    We adopted a default convention: one activator suffices, and one inhibitor suffices. Formally, each node evaluates as (any activator) AND NOT (any inhibitor). This is the weakest assumption consistent with a signed edge list — an edge asserts that the source can change the target, and nothing more. Twelve of the twenty-nine rules stand exactly as the convention produces them.

    Structural overrides

    Seventeen rules depart from the default. Each corrects a place where the edge list cannot express what the node is defined to mean, and each was argued from biology before any comparison with data.

    Stoichiometry. The apoptosome is a complex, not a switch. CASP9 = CYCS AND APAF1 AND NOT XIAP — under the default, cytochrome c would activate caspase-9 with no Apaf-1 present, making the execution module decorative.

    Constitutively present proteins. FOXO3, GSK3B, BAK1 and BCL2L1 are expressed regardless of signalling; their regulation is inhibitory. Each had exactly one weak transcriptional activator edge, which under the default would have made them p53-dependent — so that degrading KRAS could never induce BIM. Their activator requirement was removed: GSK3B = NOT (AKT1 OR MAPK1 OR MAPK3).

    Modulatory phosphorylations. Modulatory phosphorylations read as switches. AKT1 inhibits RAF1 at S259, and ERK feeds back onto both RAF and MEK. These tune signal amplitude; treated as Boolean switches they shut the MAPK arm off at baseline — impossible in a KRAS-mutant line — and generate a sustained oscillator. They are excluded from the rules and flagged as deliberately unused.

    Every override is documented inline in the rules file, and a validation tool checks each rule back against the network: it evaluates the rule across input combinations, determines whether each input actually behaves as an activator or an inhibitor, and compares that against the edge sign. A typo placing an activator under a negation is caught mechanically. It also reports every edge no rule uses, so that an edge dropped on purpose is distinguishable from one dropped by accident.

    All the rule files and the rule-validation tool can be found in our GitLab Repository.

    Attractor Analysis

    With 29 nodes the model has 2^29 — about 537 million — possible states. Clamping the three input nodes leaves roughly 67 million per condition. The question is which of those the network settles into.

    A Boolean network on n nodes is defined by a state space and a set of update functions [36, 37]. Each node takes a value in {0, 1}, and the configuration of the whole network at a given moment is the state vector

    $$x(t) = \big(x_1(t),\, x_2(t),\, \dots,\, x_n(t)\big) \in \{0,1\}^n$$

    Each node carries a Boolean function of the nodes that regulate it:

    $$x_i(t+1) = f_i\big(x_1(t),\, x_2(t),\, \dots,\, x_n(t)\big)$$

    where the k inputs are the nodes with an edge into i. The functions are built from the three operators of Boolean algebra: conjunction (AND, ∧), disjunction (OR, ∨) and negation (NOT, ¬).

    Writing A and R for the activators and inhibitors of node i, our default convention is

    $$x_i(t+1) = \bigvee_{j \in A} x_j(t) \wedge \neg\bigvee_{k \in R} x_k(t)$$

    with the convention that an empty disjunction under the negation is false, and that a node with no activators drops the first term. The alternatives explored in the screen replace a disjunction by a conjunction in one or other position — for instance requiring all activators rather than any one.

    The rule for BAD illustrates how a truth table becomes a function. Reading the rows whose output is one and applying De Morgan's law:

    $$f_{\mathrm{BAD}} \;=\; \neg\big(x_{\mathrm{AKT1}} \vee x_{\mathrm{RAF1}}\big) \;=\; \neg x_{\mathrm{AKT1}} \wedge \neg x_{\mathrm{RAF1}}$$

    The two update schemes differ in how many functions are applied per step. Synchronous updating applies all of them, giving a deterministic map [37]:

    $$x(t+1) = F\big(x(t)\big) = \big(f_1(x(t)),\, \dots,\, f_n(x(t))\big)$$

    Asynchronous updating applies one function, chosen at random, and leaves the rest unchanged [38, 39]:

    $$x_i(t+1) = f_i\big(x(t)\big), \qquad x_j(t+1) = x_j(t) \;\; \text{for all } j \neq i$$

    A fixed point satisfies

    $$F(x^{*}) = x^{*} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad f_i(x^{*}) = x^{*}_i \;\; \text{for every } i$$

    which contains no reference to update order — the formal reason fixed points are identical under both schemes, while cyclic attractors are not.

    A perturbation is a network reduction rather than a choice of starting state. Clamping node c to a value v substitutes that value into every function, leaving a Boolean network on the remaining n − 1 nodes:

    $$\tilde{f}_i \;=\; f_i\big|_{x_c = v}$$

    Our four conditions are therefore four distinct reduced networks. One consequence is worth naming: clamping MDM2 removes the p53–MDM2 negative feedback loop from the dynamics entirely.

    Finally, a trap space is a subcube of the state space — a partial assignment fixing some nodes and leaving others free — that is closed under the update, so that no trajectory beginning inside it can leave [40]. Value percolation computes the smallest trap space containing the clamped subspace. When that trap space fixes every node, it is a single state, and that state is provably the unique attractor of the reduced network.

    Updating the nodes turns the state space into a directed graph: each state points to the states reachable from it in one step. This is the state transition diagram [41]. An attractor is a set of states the system can move within but never leave — formally a terminal strongly connected component of that graph [38]. A fixed point is an attractor of one state, where every rule already agrees with the current values. A cyclic attractor is a larger set the system circulates through indefinitely. Attractors are what a Boolean model predicts; everything else is transient.

    We update asynchronously: one randomly chosen node per step, rather than all nodes at once. Synchronous updating assumes every reaction takes the same time, which is false — transcribing p21 and phosphorylating BAD differ by orders of magnitude — and it manufactures oscillations that are artefacts of that assumption. Asynchronous updating makes no timing claim at all. Fixed points are identical under both schemes; only cyclic attractors differ.

    Sampling random starting states is the usual way to find attractors [39], but it proves nothing: 120 samples out of 67 million covers two millionths of the space. We therefore used value percolation, which is exact. Clamp the inputs, then repeatedly fix any node whose function has become constant over every assignment of its still-free inputs, and iterate. The result is the smallest subspace closed under the update — a region no trajectory can leave.

    In all four conditions, percolation fixed all 29 nodes within six rounds. Each condition therefore has a single fixed point, it is the only attractor, and it is reachable from every one of the 67 million initial states. This is a proof rather than a sample, and it has a useful consequence: initial conditions cannot affect the outcome. No cyclic attractors exist anywhere in the model. Random-start sampling was run alongside and agreed in every case.

    The control attractor was not imposed — it emerges. It describes a proliferating A549 cell: constitutive KRAS and ERK signalling, active AKT, both mitochondrial guards intact, XIAP restraining the caspases, p53 held off by MDM2, and no caspase activity. A rule set that could not produce this would have been wrong before any perturbation was tested, so the control condition doubles as a check on the logic.

    Five rules admit a defensible alternative. Rather than choose among them by argument, we enumerated every combination and scored all of them against criteria fixed in advance.

    The five rules with a defensible alternative form
    Rule Node Form A Form B
    R1 BCL2L1 any antagonist suffices needs a sensitiser and an activator
    R2 BBC3 TP53 or FOXO3 TP53 only
    R3 CDKN1A all inhibitors veto MDM2 only
    R4 BAX, BAK1 both guards must go either guard suffices
    R5 TP53 either suppressor blocks both needed

    The five undetermined rules and their alternatives. Enumerating every combination gives 32 variants; four satisfy all pre-registered criteria. R1, R3 and R5 are resolved by the screen — R2 and R4 are not.

    The criteria were written to a file, reviewed, and frozen before any variant was run. They cover the control condition — a rule set that cannot produce a viable proliferating cell is rejected before any perturbation is considered — and the MDM2-degraded condition, where the Nutlin proteomics gives directional constraints. Criteria are directional only: a Boolean node cannot be compared against a fold change, so we ask whether a node is on or off, never by how much. We go into detail about the PROTEOMICS data further on.

    Thirty-two variants, four conditions each, every attractor computed by exact percolation. Four variants satisfy all criteria. R1, R3 and R5 are determined; R2 and R4 are not.

    Every failure occurs in the MDM2-degraded condition, in three modes. Sixteen variants fail because TP53 = NOT (MDM2 OR MDM4) with MDM4 clamped on makes p53 permanently inactive, so MDM2 degradation is inert. This is the Boolean abstraction breaking rather than a bad rule: two dosage-dependent suppressors of one target cannot be represented as an on-off switch, and the failure was anticipated in the criteria file before it occurred. Eight more fail because a curation-effort-5 edge (MAPK1 inhibiting CDKN1A) vetoes a curation-effort-3452 edge (p53 inducing CDKN1A), so p21 cannot rise while ERK is active. Four fail because any single BH3-only protein clears BCL-XL, contradicting the observed rise in BCL-XL under MDM2 inhibition.

    The screen is weakly determining, and we say so. Roughly thirteen binary constraints remain after the KRAS arm was withdrawn, and many are correlated — CASP3, CASP7 and CASP9 move together, and p53 drives both p21 and PUMA. The effective number of independent constraints is nearer four. Two rules coming out undetermined is the expected result, not a defect.

    R1 deserves a note rather than a silent resolution. The screen requires form B, in which BCL-XL is cleared only by a sensitiser and an activator together. But BAD is the canonical high-affinity BCL-XL antagonist, so form A is arguably the better-supported binding biology. The data needs form B and the binding literature leans toward form A; the tension is real and is reported rather than resolved.

    All the pre-registered criteria, the full 32-row grid and the machine-readable screen output can be found in our GitLab Repository.

    Comparison with Proteomics

    The model's nodes were mapped onto a published nine-drug A549 proteomics panel, of which the Nutlin arm constrains the MDM2 perturbation.

    The Nutlin arm behaves as the p53 module predicts. MDM2 rises 7.4-fold, p53 4.8-fold, p21 4.4-fold, with PUMA, BAX, BAK1 and APAF1 up more modestly. The MAPK arm does not move at all, which independently supports the decision to drop the edges between p53 and ERK. Critically, CASP3, CASP7 and XIAP are unchanged: the apoptotic machinery is primed but not fired. MDM2 inhibition alone produces arrest, not death, and that is the single most useful constraint the data provides.

    One observation contradicts a retained edge. Our network has p53 repressing BCL2L1, yet BCL-XL protein rises 1.7-fold under Nutlin. We report the discrepancy rather than removing the edge.

    The KRAS arm is unconstrained. We examined a published RAS-degradation experiment in A549 [42] but did not use it. In that dataset KRAS is not significantly reduced (0.91-fold, p = 0.49) while HRAS falls to 0.46-fold, and RAS paralogues share most of their tryptic peptides, so the absence of downstream change is consistent with two incompatible readings: that KRAS degradation does nothing here, or that KRAS was never meaningfully degraded. A criterion built on an ambiguous null would have forced the model to be insensitive to KRAS loss, encoding a possible experimental artefact as a structural feature of a model built to predict what KRAS degradation does. The KRAS-degraded and combination outcomes are therefore predictions, untested by any data in hand.

    Boolean nodes represent activity, not abundance. Most of the model's kinase edges are phosphorylation events, and total protein levels barely move when a kinase is inhibited. Only the transcriptional and stability layer is testable by shotgun proteomics at all. Three nodes — BCL2L11, PMAIP1 and MDM4 — went undetected in both datasets and are unconstrained by any criterion. And a Boolean attractor is a steady state whereas the measurements are single timepoints, so agreement in direction is the most that can be claimed.

    Bortezomib and seven other drugs in the panel remain deliberately unused, held back as the only genuine out-of-sample test available.

    Verdict

    Three results hold across every rule combination we examined, including the twenty-eight that fail the criteria. The control never dies. MDM2 degradation alone never produces apoptosis — in none of the 32 variants. And combined degradation reaches apoptosis in 28 of 32. The first two are the model's most robust statements precisely because no rule choice we tested can overturn them

    The mechanism the model proposes is a coincidence detector at BCL-XL. MCL1 is lost under either single agent — p53 induces NOXA, and losing ERK removes its stabilising phosphorylation — so MCL1 cannot distinguish one intervention from two. BCL-XL can, because clearing it requires BAD released by the loss of AKT and PUMA or NOXA induced by p53. Degrading MDM2 supplies the second without the first; degrading KRAS supplies the first without the second. Only both together clear the mitochondrial barrier.

    But the synergy claim is narrower than the 28-of-32 figure suggests. True synergy — the combination killing where neither single agent does — occurs in only 4 of 32 variants. In 24 variants KRAS degradation alone is already sufficient and MDM2 degradation contributes nothing. The synergy therefore rests on two specific claims holding together: that BCL-XL integrates both arms, and that PUMA induction is p53-dependent rather than also driven by FOXO3.

    That second claim is the model's open question and it is sharply defined. Degrading KRAS switches off AKT, which releases FOXO3 from nuclear exclusion. If FOXO3 can then induce PUMA, a p53-independent activator appears, BCL-XL falls without p53 ever activating, and the combination rationale collapses into a KRAS monotherapy result. The screen cannot settle this because FOXO3 is inactive in the only condition the criteria constrain.

    The discriminating experiment

    Measure PUMA induction after KRAS degradation in p53-intact A549, with no MDM2 agent, alongside a FOXO3 activity readout to confirm the arm engaged. PUMA rising favours the FOXO3 route and predicts that KRAS degradation kills on its own; PUMA unchanged supports p53-dependence and predicts the combination is required. A FOXO3 knockdown in the same background tests the rule directly. Note that A549 carries KRAS G12S, so the common allele-selective tool compounds do not apply and knockdown or a pan-RAS approach is needed.

    What this does and does not establish

    The network, the exact attractor analysis and the count of variants consistent with the data are results. The synergy prediction is a hypothesis generated under assumptions we have named.

    It should be stated plainly that no part of this is validation. Consistency with one drug arm, using rules chosen after inspecting where the model and the data disagreed, constrains a model without confirming it. Both datasets proved weaker than they first appeared — Nutlin inhibits MDM2 rather than degrading it. The combination arm, which is the actual question, has not been run by anyone.

    What the model does deliver is a network in which every edge carries its evidence, an exact statement of what that network implies, and a single inexpensive measurement that would decide between the two interpretations still standing.
