Introduction

Education lays the groundwork for the future, by inspiring curiosity, growth and the drive to answer complex inquiries. A central pillar of the iGEM Thessaloniki 2026 team’s mission was to deliver accessible, high-quality scientific education to diverse audiences, across different ages, experience levels and interests. Our goal was to inspire individuals to enter the world of STEM, especially synthetic biology, and equip them with valuable knowledge and skills. More specifically, we aimed to introduce kindergarten and primary students to science through interactive games, challenge high schoolers with complex concepts, hands-on lab work, and career orientation guidance, as well as empower university students and young adults through targeted skill development, most notably through a consistent series of specialized workshops. Additionally, we promoted community inclusion and health literacy among senior citizens by helping them understand modern therapeutics. On this page, we have thoroughly mapped our actions and their lasting impact, creating a clear foundation for future iGEM teams to learn from, adapt, and elevate.

Lab Discovery Day at AUTh Med School

    Overview

    Target Group: High school students from various regions of Greece with an interest in science but limited exposure to real-world research.

    Educational Goals: To bridge the gap between school-level theory and genuine research practice, introduce the structure of an interdisciplinary research team, and teach foundational bioinformatics tools (UniProt, AlphaFold).

    Activity Design: A structured presentation regarding iGEM team organization, project design, and workflows. It included interactive discussions, live demonstrations, and a guided computational exploration of protein structure prediction and analysis using UniProt and AlphaFold.

    Impact: Students responded strongly to the practical elements, which directly challenged their passive understanding of "research" and sparked curiosity about STEM careers. The team realized that experiential, interactive learning is far more effective than theoretical lectures, guiding them to reduce passive content and prioritize hands-on elements in future outreach.

    Inspiring Young Minds

      Overview-Stakeholders

      Ms. Tatsi, an experienced biologist and school counselor, provided critical insights on aligning our educational outreach with high school students' needs, emphasizing their strong interest in human physiology and pathology. She highlighted how university entrance exam stress limits students' engagement with experimental biology, prompting a frequent demand for direct interaction with university departments to understand higher education research. In response, we structured our school visit around an introduction to undergraduate interdisciplinary research, three interactive laboratory experiments, and a dedicated Q&A session to bridge the gap between secondary and higher education.

      As part of our iGEM project's Human Practices (check our Human Practices page), we consulted with educational psychologist Ms. Poimenidou to design a responsible, age-appropriate high school outreach activity addressing STEM pathways and exam-related anxiety. Her expert feedback guided us to target junior students using low-risk, interactive tools — such as fictional scenarios and anonymous questions — to ensure an emotionally safe, student-centered environment. This structured approach successfully integrated psychological insights into our educational design, maximizing the safety, feasibility, and societal relevance of our public engagement.

      To optimize our educational school visits regarding exam-related stress, we consulted with school psychologist Ms. Doka from the 1st Experimental High School of Thessaloniki. Based on her expert insights, we adapted our approach to demystify anxiety by explaining its biological mechanisms through interactive, student-centered activities that promote emotional connection. Ultimately, this collaboration enabled us to incorporate grounding exercises and connect biology to real-life experiences, effectively counteracting the stress and academic limitations imposed by demanding university entrance examinations.

      Educational Goals

      The primary objective of our school-level educational initiatives was to democratize synthetic biology and make the world of STEM accessible, engaging, and relevant to young audiences across diverse backgrounds. By shifting away from the rigid textbook memorization and exam-centric pressure that often characterize traditional curricula, we aimed to foster an environment of creative inquiry, scientific literacy, and critical thinking. Through age-appropriate, hands-on experimentation, interactive gamification, and live demonstrations, we sought to bridge the gap between abstract theoretical concepts (such as cellular biology, genetics, and bioinformatics) and tangible, real-world scientific workflows. Ultimately, our goal was to demystify university-level research, inspire students to perceive science as a collaborative and dynamic endeavor, and empower them to confidently pursue higher education and future career opportunities in biotechnology.

      Nucleotide Explorers

      Visiting the 4th Primary School of Kalamaria

      Target Group: Fifth-grade primary school students with limited prior exposure to biological sciences.

      Activity Design: An interactive, gamified session consisting of:

      • An introductory presentation of iGEM.
      • An "evolution game" based on rock-paper-scissors to simulate natural selection.
      • A live chemistry demonstration utilizing a purple cabbage indicator to show pH changes in household liquids.
      • Creative coloring activities of biomedical structures and a bracelet-making game representing complementary DNA nucleotide strands.

      Impact: The multi-dimensional activity proved that younger audiences can easily grasp complex biomedical concepts through gamification and collaborative models. It fueled student enthusiasm and led to active dialogue. For the iGEM team, this outcome reinforced the necessity of early scientific exposure and shaped their workflow to heavily rely on interactive design for future primary school visits.

      DNA Discoverers

      Intercultural Middle School of Thessaloniki

      Target Group: A highly diverse, multicultural, and multi-age group of middle school students (including refugees from Persia, Ukraine, and Syria) navigating language barriers and varying levels of academic exposure.

      Activity Design: A tailored, two-hour in-person workshop comprising:

      • An introduction to university research and the central dogma of biology.
      • A physical "Evolution Game" to simulate genetic mutations.
      • A hands-on, low-cost practical experiment where students extracted their own DNA from saliva using household items (salt, dish soap, ethanol).
      • Open dialogues breaking down scientific myths and brainstorming future research inventions.

      Impact: Practical experimentation served as an immediate equalizer that completely bypassed linguistic barriers, sparking deep critical thinking and interest in university research. Culturally diverse classrooms enriched the scientific dialogue with unique perspectives. This modular, scalable curriculum proved that low-cost tools can bring synthetic biology to underrepresented settings, prompting the school to request a lab visit and driving the iGEM team to forge long-term community relationships.

      Protein Pioneers

      Visiting the 1st High School of Kalamaria

      Target Group: Second and third-year high school students following a Health Sciences orientation, facing the pressure of university entrance exams but lacking practical lab experience.

      Activity Design: A two-hour session in the school's physics lab featuring:

      • An introduction to synthetic biology and the iGEM competition.
      • Microscopy work allowing students to operate light microscopes and observe histological slides (cerebellum, small intestine, trachea).
      • A hands-on pipetting tutorial using micropipettes and safe liquids.
      • Direct observation of plasmolysis in onion epidermal cells to illustrate osmosis.
      • A banana DNA extraction experiment utilizing affordable household items.

      Impact: heoretical biology was transformed into tangible biological phenomena, significantly enhancing the students' critical thinking and understanding. Active execution proved highly motivating, making scientific careers feel attainable. This flexible, low-cost workshop structure can easily be scaled by other teams to improve scientific literacy in resource-limited schools.
