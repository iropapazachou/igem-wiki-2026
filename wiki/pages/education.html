Introduction

Education lays the groundwork for the future, by inspiring curiosity, growth and the drive to answer complex inquiries. A central pillar of the iGEM Thessaloniki 2026 team’s mission was to deliver accessible, high-quality scientific education to diverse audiences, across different ages, experience levels and interests. Our goal was to inspire individuals to enter the world of STEM, especially synthetic biology, and equip them with valuable knowledge and skills. More specifically, we aimed to introduce kindergarten and primary students to science through interactive games, challenge high schoolers with complex concepts, hands-on lab work, and career orientation guidance, as well as empower university students and young adults through targeted skill development, most notably through a consistent series of specialized workshops. Additionally, we promoted community inclusion and health literacy among senior citizens by helping them understand modern therapeutics. On this page, we have thoroughly mapped our actions and their lasting impact, creating a clear foundation for future iGEM teams to learn from, adapt, and elevate.

Lab Discovery Day at AUTh Med School

    Overview

    Target Group: High school students from various regions of Greece with an interest in science but limited exposure to real-world research.

    Educational Goals: To bridge the gap between school-level theory and genuine research practice, introduce the structure of an interdisciplinary research team, and teach foundational bioinformatics tools (UniProt, AlphaFold).

    Activity Design: A structured presentation regarding iGEM team organization, project design, and workflows. It included interactive discussions, live demonstrations, and a guided computational exploration of protein structure prediction and analysis using UniProt and AlphaFold.

    Impact: Students responded strongly to the practical elements, which directly challenged their passive understanding of "research" and sparked curiosity about STEM careers. The team realized that experiential, interactive learning is far more effective than theoretical lectures, guiding them to reduce passive content and prioritize hands-on elements in future outreach.

    Inspiring Young Minds

      Overview-Stakeholders

      Ms. Tatsi, an experienced biologist and school counselor, provided critical insights on aligning our educational outreach with high school students' needs, emphasizing their strong interest in human physiology and pathology. She highlighted how university entrance exam stress limits students' engagement with experimental biology, prompting a frequent demand for direct interaction with university departments to understand higher education research. In response, we structured our school visit around an introduction to undergraduate interdisciplinary research, three interactive laboratory experiments, and a dedicated Q&A session to bridge the gap between secondary and higher education.

      As part of our iGEM project's Human Practices (check our Human Practices page), we consulted with educational psychologist Ms. Poimenidou to design a responsible, age-appropriate high school outreach activity addressing STEM pathways and exam-related anxiety. Her expert feedback guided us to target junior students using low-risk, interactive tools — such as fictional scenarios and anonymous questions — to ensure an emotionally safe, student-centered environment. This structured approach successfully integrated psychological insights into our educational design, maximizing the safety, feasibility, and societal relevance of our public engagement.

      To optimize our educational school visits regarding exam-related stress, we consulted with school psychologist Ms. Doka from the 1st Experimental High School of Thessaloniki. Based on her expert insights, we adapted our approach to demystify anxiety by explaining its biological mechanisms through interactive, student-centered activities that promote emotional connection. Ultimately, this collaboration enabled us to incorporate grounding exercises and connect biology to real-life experiences, effectively counteracting the stress and academic limitations imposed by demanding university entrance examinations.

      Educational Goals

      The primary objective of our school-level educational initiatives was to democratize synthetic biology and make the world of STEM accessible, engaging, and relevant to young audiences across diverse backgrounds. By shifting away from the rigid textbook memorization and exam-centric pressure that often characterize traditional curricula, we aimed to foster an environment of creative inquiry, scientific literacy, and critical thinking. Through age-appropriate, hands-on experimentation, interactive gamification, and live demonstrations, we sought to bridge the gap between abstract theoretical concepts (such as cellular biology, genetics, and bioinformatics) and tangible, real-world scientific workflows. Ultimately, our goal was to demystify university-level research, inspire students to perceive science as a collaborative and dynamic endeavor, and empower them to confidently pursue higher education and future career opportunities in biotechnology.

      Nucleotide Explorers

      Visiting the 4th Primary School of Kalamaria

      Target Group: Fifth-grade primary school students with limited prior exposure to biological sciences.

      Activity Design: An interactive, gamified session consisting of:

      • An introductory presentation of iGEM.
      • An "evolution game" based on rock-paper-scissors to simulate natural selection.
      • A live chemistry demonstration utilizing a purple cabbage indicator to show pH changes in household liquids.
      • Creative coloring activities of biomedical structures and a bracelet-making game representing complementary DNA nucleotide strands.

      Impact: The multi-dimensional activity proved that younger audiences can easily grasp complex biomedical concepts through gamification and collaborative models. It fueled student enthusiasm and led to active dialogue. For the iGEM team, this outcome reinforced the necessity of early scientific exposure and shaped their workflow to heavily rely on interactive design for future primary school visits.

      DNA Discoverers

      Intercultural Middle School of Thessaloniki

      Target Group: A highly diverse, multicultural, and multi-age group of middle school students (including refugees from Persia, Ukraine, and Syria) navigating language barriers and varying levels of academic exposure.

      Activity Design: A tailored, two-hour in-person workshop comprising:

      • An introduction to university research and the central dogma of biology.
      • A physical "Evolution Game" to simulate genetic mutations.
      • A hands-on, low-cost practical experiment where students extracted their own DNA from saliva using household items (salt, dish soap, ethanol).
      • Open dialogues breaking down scientific myths and brainstorming future research inventions.

      Impact: Practical experimentation served as an immediate equalizer that completely bypassed linguistic barriers, sparking deep critical thinking and interest in university research. Culturally diverse classrooms enriched the scientific dialogue with unique perspectives. This modular, scalable curriculum proved that low-cost tools can bring synthetic biology to underrepresented settings, prompting the school to request a lab visit and driving the iGEM team to forge long-term community relationships.

      Protein Pioneers

      Visiting the 1st High School of Kalamaria

      Target Group: Second and third-year high school students following a Health Sciences orientation, facing the pressure of university entrance exams but lacking practical lab experience.

      Activity Design: A two-hour session in the school's physics lab featuring:

      • An introduction to synthetic biology and the iGEM competition.
      • Microscopy work allowing students to operate light microscopes and observe histological slides (cerebellum, small intestine, trachea).
      • A hands-on pipetting tutorial using micropipettes and safe liquids.
      • Direct observation of plasmolysis in onion epidermal cells to illustrate osmosis.
      • A banana DNA extraction experiment utilizing affordable household items.

      Impact: heoretical biology was transformed into tangible biological phenomena, significantly enhancing the students' critical thinking and understanding. Active execution proved highly motivating, making scientific careers feel attainable. This flexible, low-cost workshop structure can easily be scaled by other teams to improve scientific literacy in resource-limited schools.

      Cellular Innovators

      We conducted a series of workshops focused on computational biology, designing each session to introduce participants to modern bioinformatic tools and molecular modeling software. To ensure the continuous improvement of these educational initiatives, we distributed evaluation forms to participants at the conclusion of each workshop, allowing us to gather valuable feedback and identify specific areas for refinement in subsequent sessions. Recognizing that each audience possessed a unique scientific background, we dynamically adapted our content and teaching depth to match their prior knowledge—ranging from foundational molecular biology introductions for secondary school students to advanced command-line operations for specialized undergraduates. This iterative approach, combining participant feedback with a careful consideration of their academic baseline, allowed us to successfully translate complex computational algorithms into accessible, highly engaging, and skills-based student workflows.

      Introduction to 3D Protein Modeling Workshop (AUTh School of Medicine)

      Target Group: Undergraduate medical and science students participating in the 15th Scientific Congress of the AUTh School of Medicine.

      Educational Goal: Our goal was to introduce computational tools for predicting 3D structures to students, explain confidence metrics, and demonstrate how amino acid sequences dictate protein folding and function in disease research.

      Activity Design: Organized in collaboration with the FunPATh research group, this workshop featured an introductory folding lecture followed by a hands-on session. Students retrieved amino acid sequences from the UniProt database, ran real-time 3D predictions using AlphaFold 2 and 3, and completed optional structural evaluation tasks for an award.

      Impact: This session directly supported dry lab educational goals, giving undergraduates the freedom to explore proteins of personal interest. Evaluation forms indicated high engagement but highlighted a need for more hands-on time, teaching the team how to better translate complex algorithms into engaging student workflows.

      Exploring Genetic Mutations Workshop (12th ACSTAC)

      Target Group: Late middle school and early high school students attending the Anatolia College Science and Technology Annual Conference.

      Educational Goal:To provide a clear introduction to molecular biology, central dogma, the biological pathways of cancer (PTEN gene), and show how computational biology helps diagnose and treat real-world diseases.

      Activity Design:A hands-on computational workshop guiding students through multiple professional bioinformatics platforms, including UniProt, AlphaFold Database, ENSEMBL Genome Browser (for sequence localization), BLAST/BLAT (for spotting missense mutations), and Tumor Portal.

      Impact:The session successfully advanced public scientific literacy by making complex genomics accessible to secondary school students. Participants independently applied their new bioinformatics skills to examine genes, proving that presenting research tools as an interactive exercise cultivates critical thinking and curiosity.

      AlphaFold: Structure Driven Medicine Workshop (32nd SCHMS)

      Target Group: Undergraduate medical students at the 32nd Scientific Congress of Hellenic Medical Students.

      Educational Goal: To introduce medical future-professionals to artificial intelligence modeling (AlphaFold) for physiological and pathological conditions, and show how computational tools minimize wet lab trial-and-error.

      Activity Design:A theoretical lecture outlining structural biology techniques and UniProt utilities, followed by a practical laboratory session. Students utilized AlphaFold 2 and 3 to visualize protein complexes, and were given an analytical task to rank three distinct molecular structures in descending order of predictive confidence.

      Impact:Medical students actively engaged with structural biology, gaining a practical appreciation for how computational modeling guides modern clinical and pharmaceutical design. The experience also strengthened the iGEM team's own dry lab capacities and refined their academic presentation methods.

      3D Protein Modeling 2.0 (1st Aristotle Innovation Forum)

      Target Group: Advanced participants and students looking to dive deeper into synthetic and computational structural biology.

      Educational Goal: To bridge basic sequence analysis with advanced molecular visualization, teaching participants how to analyze structural interface dynamics, chemical bonds, and interaction dynamics.

      Activity Design:Building on prior database workshops, this advanced event introduced command-line operations using UCSF ChimeraX visualization software alongside AlphaFold 3 predictions. To ensure smooth workflow, the team equipped participants with a comprehensive, step-by-step command guide.

      Educational Award for the workshop winners

      Following our workshop series, we introduced an optional bioinformatics challenge to transition participants from passive listeners into active researchers. Students were asked to independently experiment with the computational tools we presented, such as UniProt and AlphaFold, and submit a structured analysis report. Our team thoroughly reviewed the submissions, evaluating each student's precision, understanding of structural metrics, and scientific depth. The authors of the top-performing reports were selected for the grand prize, which was an exclusive, hands-on educational day at the Biological Chemistry Laboratory of AUTh Medical School. This competitive process successfully encouraged independent scientific engagement and allowed us to reward the most dedicated participants with a real-world research experience.

      Target Group:The selected challenge winners, comprising top-performing students who demonstrated exceptional skills in their bioinformatics reports.

      Educational Goal: To offer firsthand exposure to a professional biomedical environment, showcase real-world interdisciplinary collaboration, and bridge the gap between dry lab theory and wet lab benchwork.

      Activity Design:The winners spent a full educational day at our headquarters inside the Biological Chemistry Laboratory of the AUTh Medical School. Their immersive schedule included shadowing our researchers, observing active wet lab experiments, attending core team meetings, and participating in a dry lab strategic session focused on molecular modeling tools.

      Impact:By stepping inside a real academic lab, the students experienced the authentic workflow of a student-led research team. Witnessing how dry lab predictive models directly shape wet lab protocols gave them invaluable practical insight, fueling their passion for scientific inquiry and future STEM careers.

      Target Group:University students from the Law Debate Society "Tharsin" of the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki, alongside law and psychology students.

      Aristotle University x Fire Academy Cooperation Establishment

        Overview-Stakeholders

        As part of our iGEM project's Human Practices (check our Human Practices page), we consulted with Mr. Bikas, a Senior Officer of the Hellenic Fire Corps and Expert of the European Civil Protection Mechanism, to understand real-world occupational exposure, chemical environments, and safety equipment usage beyond standard literature, since firefighters are a highly vulnerable group exposed to intense, complex airborne carcinogens relevant to our lung cancer project. His expert insights on pollutant variability, behavioral psychology during crises, and chronic health consequences showed us that pollutants depend on what burns. Forest fires dilute carbon monoxide, while urban and vehicle fires heavily expose personnel to highly toxic phenols and PVC byproducts. He also highlighted the "immediate action" dilemma, in which firefighters, driven by the instinct to save lives, often neglect precautions and enter danger zones without respiratory gear. He further noted that veteran firefighters suffer from severe respiratory and cardiovascular problems due to long-term exposure, yet firefighter deaths are rarely formally researched. This collaboration enriched our educational actions with authentic, experience-based knowledge of occupational carcinogen exposure and confirmed firefighters as a primary target group, reinforcing our project's societal relevance. It also refined our Integrated Human Practices by showing that our therapeutic tool can have a life-saving impact on this high-risk community.

        iGEM Thessaloniki visit at the Firefighters School

        Target Group:Hosted in Tsotili, Kozani,in the Firefighters School facilities with the presence of Firefighter School Commander, Fire Colonel of General Duties Archontia Sereti, Fire Colonel of Medical Service Ioannis Tsavdaridis (Pathologist), Commander of Fire Stations in Municipality of Kozani ,Fire Colonel of General Duties Gkikas Kampouridis,Fire Warrant Officer of General Duties Gavreilidis Christos, Fire Sergeant of General Duties Giakoumis Angelos, the fire officers of the school and 268 firefighter cadets.

        Educational Goal:The project of iGEM Thessaloniki 2023 team was relevant to helping forests regrow after a fire using synthetic biology. Every summer in Greece there are dangerous wildfires and the Hellenic Fire Corps fight their best to protect the citizens and the environment. However they often perform their duty at the cost of their health or even life. Nowadays due to climate change wildfires spread all over the world and harm the environment. However not only wildfires but civil fires can harm the firefighters.One of the many health problems firefighters may deal with is lung cancer due to the smoke which they inhale. Having all these in mind, representatives of the team approached the Firefighters Academy through the Commander of Firefighters School , Fire Colonel of General Duties Archontia Sereti and requested to present to the cadets a systematic analysis on the possibilities of firefighters developing lung cancer. The result was a one day event to be organized at the facilities of the Firefighters School Department in Tsotili Kozani

        Activity Design: In the beginning of the session,Fire Colonel of Medical Service Ioannis Tsavdaridis presented the multiple ways which can harm the health of firefighters and referred to actual incidents which resulted in casualties. Afterwards our team presented -based on literature review carried by the team’ s medical students- the chemicals and particles in fire smoke, the bioindicators which are found high in firefighters’ tissues and indicate possible carcinogenesis, trials using CT in lungs and RAD criteria and they also recommended firefighters still keep the breathing filters on during autopsy despite the obvious reason not to do so based on experiments on mice. In the end, Fire Warrant Officer of General Duties Gavreilidis Christos, Fire Sergeant of General Duties Giakoumis Angelos presented the evolution of the personal protective equipment and breathing devices.

        Impact:Cadets gained a vital understanding of the immediate health risks of their profession. Academy officers decided to reform their safety guidelines to match the provided scientific data and invited the team for a series of future lectures or even clinical trials. The event launched a formal cooperation between the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki and the Fire Academy, aligned with the national "Active Battle" law for lab-to-field integrations launching a formal cooperation between AUTh and the Fire Academy.
