Introduction

Education lays the groundwork for the future, by inspiring curiosity, growth and the drive to answer complex inquiries. A central pillar of the iGEM Thessaloniki 2026 team’s mission was to deliver accessible, high-quality scientific education to diverse audiences, across different ages, experience levels and interests. Our goal was to inspire individuals to enter the world of STEM, especially synthetic biology, and equip them with valuable knowledge and skills. More specifically, we aimed to introduce kindergarten and primary students to science through interactive games, challenge high schoolers with complex concepts, hands-on lab work, and career orientation guidance, as well as empower university students and young adults through targeted skill development, most notably through a consistent series of specialized workshops. Additionally, we promoted community inclusion and health literacy among senior citizens by helping them understand modern therapeutics.

On this page, we have thoroughly mapped our actions and their lasting impact, creating a clear foundation for future iGEM teams to learn from, adapt, and elevate.

Lab Discovery Day at AUTh Med School

    Overview

    Within the framework of outreach initiatives of Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (AUTH), high school students from across Greece visit the university to gain exposure to academic life, scientific research, and modern technological applications. In this context, the iGEM Thessaloniki team engaged with high school students from various regions who had limited prior exposure to synthetic biology and related computational research tools. The session aimed to bridge the gap between school-level education and scientific practice by presenting research as a structured process within the iGEM competition.

    Members of iGEM Thessaloniki delivered an educational outreach presentation to high school students, aiming to introduce them to the fundamentals of synthetic biology and the structure of a real research team. The presentation covered how a student-led research team operates, including team organization, project design, wet and dry lab workflows, and engagement with society. In addition to the theoretical framework, students were introduced to modern bioinformatics tools such as UniProt and AlphaFold, gaining hands-on insight into how protein information is retrieved and how protein structures can be predicted and analyzed. The session emphasized the connection between biology, computation, and engineering, highlighting real-world applications and research practices.

    Students responded more strongly to practical demonstrations than to purely theoretical content. The concept of an interdisciplinary research team (wet lab, dry lab, human practices) was new and particularly impactful. Tools such as AlphaFold and UniProt significantly increased engagement and curiosity. There was a noticeable gap in understanding what "research" actually involves and how it connects to academic and professional paths. Interactive elements (live demos, discussion) greatly improved participation and comprehension.

    The implementation of the activity was based on a structured presentation that progressively introduced students to the concept of synthetic-biology research through the example of iGEM Thessaloniki. The content covered team formation, project design, laboratory and computational workflows, and the importance of connecting research with societal needs. Students were introduced to the fundamentals of synthetic biology and the organization of a research team, followed by a guided exploration of tools such as UniProt and AlphaFold. This approach enabled a transition from theoretical understanding to practical engagement with real scientific workflows.

    The outcome of the activity was a clearer understanding of how synthetic-biology research is conducted in iGEM, alongside increased interest in interdisciplinary fields that combine biology, computation, and engineering. The use of live demonstrations and interactive discussion significantly enhanced engagement and facilitated comprehension of complex concepts.

    The experience provided valuable feedback regarding the effectiveness of different outreach strategies. It became evident that experiential and interactive learning approaches are more effective than purely theoretical presentations. Based on this, future activities will focus more on hands-on elements, discussion-based learning, and active participation, while reducing passive theoretical content. Additionally, the importance of adapting the level of explanation to the audience was reinforced, guiding improvements in communication and overall presentation design for subsequent outreach actions.

      Educational Goals

      The primary objective of our school-level educational initiatives was to democratize synthetic biology and make the world of STEM accessible, engaging, and relevant to young audiences across diverse backgrounds. By shifting away from the rigid textbook memorization and exam-centric pressure that often characterize traditional curricula, we aimed to foster an environment of creative inquiry, scientific literacy, and critical thinking. Through age-appropriate, hands-on experimentation, interactive gamification, and live demonstrations, we sought to bridge the gap between abstract theoretical concepts (such as cellular biology, genetics, and bioinformatics) and tangible, real-world scientific workflows. Ultimately, our goal was to demystify university-level research, inspire students to perceive science as a collaborative and dynamic endeavor, and empower them to confidently pursue higher education and future career opportunities in biotechnology.

      Little Aminos

      Visiting the 5th Kindergarten of Kalamaria

      The audience of this educational activity was composed of children attending the 5th Kindergarten of Kalamaria. Participants, aged 4–6 years, were at the earliest stage of formal education, with no prior exposure to biological science. Our goal was to offer a first, playful encounter with the microscopic world, introducing the concept of microbes, the body's natural defences and the importance of hygiene through activities designed to engage rather than instruct, and to spark curiosity rather than convey facts.

      The purpose of this educational activity was to introduce young children to the invisible world of microbes and to the basic principles of how the body protects itself, using hands-on demonstrations, collaborative activities and creative play. More specifically, the activity included:

      • A brief, accessible introduction to microbes, explaining that the world is full of living things too small to see, and that while some can make us sick, many are harmless or even beneficial. This distinction was presented as the foundation for understanding why hygiene matters.
      • A glitter demonstration to illustrate microbial transmission. Glitter was applied to the children's hands to represent microbes, and they were then invited to touch surfaces and each other's hands, making the invisible routes of spread — from surfaces to hands, and from person to person — immediately and visually apparent.
      • A hand-washing song, in which the correct technique for washing hands was taught through a song the children could follow along with and repeat. This activity combined physical practice with a memorable format suited to the age group.
      • A discussion on skin as the body's first line of defence, focusing on why we should never touch the "unprotected" eyes, nose, mouth, ears, or any open wound with unwashed hands. Children were then shown histological slides of skin under a microscope, allowing them to see the structural reality behind the concept.
      • A discussion on airborne transmission, explaining how coughing and sneezing can spread microbes through the air. This led naturally into a conversation about how we breathe and how the lungs work, accompanied by microscopic images of lung tissue for the children to observe.
      • Colouring sheets depicting a mixed scene of microbes and laboratory objects. The children were invited to identify the microbes among the other objects and colour them freely using their own imagination.

      What participants learned. Through this activity, participants had the opportunity to encounter the concept of microbes for the first time: learning that invisible living things surround us, and that our everyday habits, particularly handwashing, are a meaningful form of protection. They were introduced to the idea that the skin protects us, while the nose, the mouth and the eyes are all entry points that require care, and they observed real biological structures of skin and lung tissue under the microscope, connecting abstract concepts to visible images. Most importantly, the children were able to ask questions and share observations freely, discovering that curiosity about the natural world is something scientists share with them.

      What we learned. This activity was equally instructive for our team. It required us to translate concepts that we engage with daily in a highly technical context into language and experiences accessible to children who had never encountered a microscope, basic biological concepts, or the word "microbe" before. We observed that even at this age, children respond with immediate engagement when an abstract idea is made physically tangible: the glitter demonstration, in particular, produced a visible moment of recognition that no verbal explanation could have achieved as quickly. We also learned that movement, song and creative activity are not merely ways of entertaining a young audience, but genuine vehicles for learning at this developmental stage.

      Insights: what changed in our understanding. This activity deepened our appreciation for the gap between scientific literacy and scientific curiosity. The children had no background in biology, but their enthusiasm for the microscope slides, their delight in identifying microbes on the colouring sheets, and their unprompted questions about how the body works made clear that curiosity precedes knowledge, and that early educational encounters with science can shape the relationship a child develops with it for years afterward. It also reinforced a lesson we had begun to draw from our primary school visit: that the most effective communication of scientific ideas is almost always the most concrete, the most participatory and the most joyful.

      Our visit to the 5th Kindergarten of Kalamaria confirmed that meaningful science education can begin earlier than formal curricula typically assume, and that the tools required are ones that any team willing to invest in their design can provide. The success of the glitter transmission demonstration reinforced our commitment to building outreach activities around physical analogies rather than abstract explanations. The hand-washing song demonstrated that repetition through play produces retention in ways that instruction alone cannot. And the children's engagement with the microscope slides reminded us that access to real scientific instruments, even briefly and informally, can make a lasting impression. We left more convinced than before that outreach at the earliest educational levels is not a peripheral activity but a genuine contribution to the culture of scientific thinking.

      Nucleotide Explorers

      Visiting the 4th Primary School of Kalamaria

      The audience of this educational activity was composed of fifth grade students of the 4th Primary School of Kalamaria. Participants were young students with limited exposure to the world of biological sciences. Our goal was to provide a smooth and engaging introduction to biomedical science by presenting fundamental concepts of molecular, cellular, and evolutionary biology in an accessible, interactive, and enjoyable way.

      The purpose of this educational activity was to provide young students in primary education with an opportunity to understand basic principles of biological science and to be introduced to the spirit of biomedical practice and research methodology. More specifically, the activity included:

      • A presentation of iGEM Thessaloniki in the context of the international iGEM Competition, focusing on the team's research interests and on the students who compose it.
      • An introductory theoretical presentation on basic concepts of cellular and molecular biology, including cells, genetic material, its gene content, and its structural organization within cells.
      • Fun fact questions with a biological background, related to the evolutionary biology of humans as well as other species of the animal and plant kingdoms.
      • An evolution game, in which students played rock-paper-scissors in pairs and were able to progress through higher evolutionary stages, starting from fish and ultimately reaching the human species.
      • A demonstration of color-changing experiments using a chemical indicator extracted from boiled purple cabbage, following its addition to different acidic and basic solutions, such as vinegar, water, and lemon juice.
      • Coloring activities using coloring sheets that depicted structures and objects related to biomedical science, such as a light microscope, a neuron, and a human lung, with the bronchial tree represented as a branching flower-like structure.
      • A bracelet-making activity using colored beads of four different colors, each corresponding to one nucleotide base. This activity was carried out in pairs, with students attempting to construct a bracelet with a genetically complementary sequence to that of their partner.

      Two-Way Dialogue: Mutual Learning

      What Participants Learned. Through this multidimensional activity, participants had the opportunity to:

      • Be introduced to the world of cellular and molecular biology by understanding fundamental concepts of these scientific fields. More specifically, they became familiar with the concept of the cell, the genetic material whose genes contribute to various phenotypic traits, and the organization of genes into chromosomes within the cell nucleus.
      • Understand the concept of species evolution and, at the same time, develop questions and curiosity regarding the evolutionary origin of the human species.
      • Understand the concept of acidity and fundamental principles of acid-base chemistry.
      • Gain a first introduction to the basic principle of DNA nucleotide base complementarity.
      • Learn about the exciting field of synthetic biology and the contribution of an iGEM student team to this scientific field.

      Most importantly, students were able to express questions and thoughts related to science and, through a constructive and fruitful dialogue, expand their knowledge of the world of biomedical science.

      What We Learned. This educational activity was equally valuable for our team, as it provided us with insight into both the level of familiarity primary school students have with biomedical science and the way this field should be approached when working with younger audiences.

      We observed that, although young students have minimal prior exposure to biological science, their interest and enthusiasm for discovering different aspects of it, such as cellular and molecular biology, tissue organization, and genetics, were remarkably high.

      Furthermore, through the questions they raised about different areas of biomedical science, it became clear that primary school students are eager to come into contact with the world of science and with the people who contribute to it. This highlighted the importance of early exposure to scientific thinking and helped us understand how such activities can support the development of a scientific mindset from a young age.

      Insights: What Changed in Our Understanding. This activity helped us identify several important points:

      • Primary school students are eager to engage with the fundamental principles of biomedical science.
      • Engaging activities make biological concepts more accessible to young audiences compared with purely theoretical presentations.
      • Experimental demonstrations fascinate students and help them develop a stronger interest in biological science.
      • Activities that require collaboration strengthen students' social skills and highlight the cooperative spirit that lies at the core of scientific practice.

      The experience reinforced our belief that science education becomes more meaningful when students are encouraged to participate actively, ask questions, and connect scientific concepts with creative and enjoyable activities.

      Implementation: How This Activity Influenced Our Work. This educational visit influenced the way we, as an iGEM team, approach educational outreach activities for primary school students.

      First, it confirmed students' enthusiasm for biomedical science and their desire to come into contact with it. As a result, we became even more committed to designing educational visits for elementary schools.

      Second, the activity demonstrated the importance of gamification as a means of communicating relatively complex scientific concepts to students with little to no previous exposure to science. This encouraged us to design more science-based games with the broader goal of promoting scientific knowledge to younger audiences.

      Third, designing the activities around a collaborative model appeared to further increase students' enthusiasm, while also emphasizing the social nature of scientific work. Therefore, we decided to incorporate collaboration more strongly into our future outreach activities.

      Overall, this activity strengthened our commitment to making biomedical science education for primary school students more engaging, accessible, and enjoyable.

      DNA Discoverers

      Intercultural Middle School of Thessaloniki

      The target audience for this educational initiative was composed of middle school students from the Intercultural Middle School of Thessaloniki. This unique student body comprised individuals from diverse age groups and varying ethnic, national and cultural backgrounds, including students from countries such as Persia, Ukraine, and Syria. Unlike standard classrooms, these students possessed highly diverse levels of prior academic exposure and varying degrees of fluency in the host language (Greek). Our goal was to create an inclusive environment that could bypass language barriers, introduce them to the groundbreaking field of synthetic biology, and spark their interest in scientific inquiry through tailored, interactive, and highly visual learning experiences.

      The visit was conducted in person, on May 21st, and we optimized a two-hour session to carry out the following activities:

      • We introduced the iGEM Thessaloniki team and the competition framework as well as defined Synthetic Biology and shared how undergraduate students can perform scientific research at a university level.
      • We explained the way living organisms are built and mapped out how genetic information flows from DNA to mRNA and proteins. We then connected these structural concepts to evolution, discussing how organisms adapt and change over time through natural selection.
      • To demonstrate evolutionary pressure and natural selection in an engaging and fun way we played the "Evolution Game," a creative version of "Rock, Paper, Scissors". Students simulated genetic mutation and evolved into higher organism levels with each victory.
      • Moving on, we carried out a practical experiment where students extracted visible strands of their own DNA from saliva using everyday household items. This hands-on activity allowed them to apply their newly acquired knowledge regarding cell membranes and DNA properties to witness their own genetic material form in the tube.
      • Next, we led an open discussion to demystify common scientific myths, sharing engaging facts about the immense length of human DNA and the biological pathways behind our bodies' ability to process pain.
      • We concluded the session by asking the students what they would investigate or invent if they were researchers, with each member of iGEM Thessaloniki sharing their thoughts. This sparked intriguing responses from the classroom, ranging from the intersection of art, physics and neuroscience to breakthroughs in cancer research and cardiology.

      What students learned. Through these tailored demonstrations and games, the students were introduced to:

      • The fundamental hierarchy of life (cells, bacteria, genetic material) as well as the structure and properties of DNA.
      • The basic mechanics of natural selection and evolutionary adaptation via interactive play.
      • The practical steps of cell lysis and nucleic acid precipitation through a safe, self-administered DNA extraction protocol.
      • The collaborative world of university research, synthetic biology, and the inclusive nature of the global iGEM community.

      What we learned. We observed that while the students initially had limited experience with practical biology and science, introducing hands-on experiments and gamified activities made abstract concepts significantly easier for them to understand and retain.

      Many students showed a deep curiosity about their own biology, leading to numerous inquiries and spontaneous discussions about how our bodies work, particularly regarding sensory processing and genetics. This dialogue became deeply profound when the academic concepts naturally intersected with their diverse personal backgrounds and life stories.

      Moreover, the student audience asked multiple enthusiastic questions about how university students can actively participate in research, which helped us understand that exposing students to initiatives like iGEM early on makes higher education feel more accessible and exciting.

      Overall, we learned that managing a multicultural and multi-age classroom presents distinct communication and structural challenges. However, the students' genuine curiosity and high level of engagement proved that tailoring complex science for diverse audiences is not only entirely achievable, but also deeply rewarding and absolutely necessary for empowering student communities that have fewer educational opportunities.

      Insights: What Changed in Our Understanding. We observed that traditional, textbook-heavy curricula fail to capture the imagination of students who are simultaneously navigating cultural integration. Shifting to hands-on, experiential learning acts as an immediate equalizer, proving that these students are incredibly bright and capable and that educators simply need to spark their interest and build accessible communication channels to bridge the gap.

      We recognized that cultural and linguistic diversity in a classroom is a profound asset. Rather than being a barrier, it actively encourages unique questions, creative critical thinking, and unexpected analytical perspectives that enrich the scientific dialogue for everyone involved.

      Finally, our interactions highlighted that learning biology, especially neuroscience, goes far beyond academic facts; it can help individuals better understand themselves, grasp how their unique life experiences have physically shaped them, and discover paths to personal growth. This realization taught us that teaching these subjects requires emotional sensitivity and an appreciation for the deeply personal perspectives each student brings to the classroom.

      We observed that students from diverse national and linguistic backgrounds were most engaged when they could actively participate in experiments and games rather than simply listen to verbal explanations. This proved that hands-on learning acts as an immediate equalizer, committing us to designing interactive activities that successfully bypass language barriers. Moving forward, we also applied this lesson broadly by designing our educational material around active storytelling, relatable human analogies and interactive visual elements.

      We saw that even simple experiments using household items carry a significant educational impact when they connect directly to basic human biology. This encouraged us to prioritize accessible, low-cost, and reproducible demonstrations that can be implemented easily in schools with limited laboratory infrastructure or fewer educational opportunities.

      Finally, the intense enthusiasm these students showed for learning about synthetic biology and university research proved that we need to introduce scientific opportunities much earlier. This was made incredibly clear when a young student shared his contact information so we could inform him about future workshops and educational activities, and the school asked to visit our university lab. We have therefore become more intentional about using our outreach to build long-term relationships and empower underrepresented students to pursue higher education and student-led research initiatives like iGEM.

      Scalability and Reproducibility. We deliberately engineered this curriculum to serve as a modular, low-cost blueprint that can be easily replicated, scaled up, or adapted by other iGEM teams, educators, and science communicators globally.

      The backbone of our practical curriculum relies on everyday, safe household items (table salt, dish soap, ethanol), and basic game dynamics. By eliminating the need for expensive laboratory reagents, specialized hardware, or complex infrastructure, we ensured that this activity can be executed seamlessly in any standard classroom, community center, or resource-limited setting.

      We structured the two-hour workflow into independent, stackable blocks (Introduction → Theory → Game → Lab → Open Dialogue). This modularity allows future instructors to scale the timing up or down, or swap out specific topics depending on the age group, background, or time constraints of their specific audience.

      Because the core concepts are reinforced through physical movement (the evolution game), physical interaction (DNA extraction) and strong visual analogies, the curriculum does not rely solely on dense, textbook-heavy lecturing. This makes the workshop much easier to reproduce in multicultural or multi-age environments where students are navigating varying levels of language fluency.

      This initiative highlighted the real-world value of inclusive science communication. Interactive and engaging activities such as this one are crucial for encouraging young students to adopt a scientific mindset of curiosity and experimentation when the rigidity of traditional curricula may prove stifling. By tailoring our approach to a multicultural classroom with varying language fluencies, we proved that interactive games and hands-on experiments are incredibly effective at breaking down learning barriers and making an emerging field like synthetic biology accessible to everyone.

      Through such demonstrations, followed by time allocated for open discussions, diverse students realize how biology connects directly to their own lives and that university research initiatives like iGEM are not out of reach, but fully welcoming to them. Ultimately, this experiential method showed that science can build a meaningful personal connection, cultivating a deep interest for research while building a crucial, inclusive bridge from academia to society.

      Protein Pioneers

      Visiting the 1st High School of Kalamaria

      The audience of this educational activity was composed of third and second year students of the 1st General High School of Kalamaria, who followed the Health Sciences orientation or were set to follow it the following year accordingly. Participants were students with a vested interest in biological sciences, who, however, had limited to no experience with biology outside of the strictly theoretical and rigid school curriculum, in the stressful context of the looming university entrance exams. Our goal was to reinvigorate their interest in learning by applying their knowledge in engaging, practical experiments, as well as introduce them to synthetic biology and the work of iGEM teams.

      This visit was carried out in person on the 7th of March at the 1st General High School's school lab, which currently serves as a physics classroom. In the span of two school hours we directed the following activities:

      • A presentation of iGEM Thessaloniki and its work in the context of the iGEM Competition, familiarizing the students with synthetic biology and with student teams they would have the opportunity to join in university.
      • Observation of histological slides under the microscope: cerebellum, small intestine, and trachea. After an introductory presentation to medical histology, the students had the opportunity to operate a microscope for the first time, and understand how human cells and tissues structurally inform organ function.
      • Introduction to pipetting: we taught the students how to use a micropipette, an essential piece of molecular biology laboratory equipment which they had never even seen up close before. Each of them had the chance to practice pipetting with safe liquids, getting to know safe handling of this equipment and getting a taste of mol-bio everyday lab routine.
      • Next, we observed allium cepa (onion) epidermal cells under the microscope, both before and after plasmolysis with table salt. This comparison helped students better understand osmosis, a phenomenon they learned in theory but which some had trouble with, by witnessing the cellular effects of its biological applications.
      • Finally, we concluded our visit with a DNA extraction experiment. We used common household items (dish soap, table salt, ethanol) to extract the DNA of banana fruit. What this experiment offered was the chance for the students to apply their knowledge about the cellular membrane and the properties of DNA to rationalize and understand the experimental protocol, with the additional bonus of a visible result.

      What Participants Learned. Through the various demonstrations and experiments, students were introduced to:

      • The basic operation of a light microscope and the observation of biological tissues and cells.
      • The relationship between cellular structure and organ function through the study of histological specimens.
      • The proper use of micropipettes and the importance of precision in laboratory work.
      • The biological principles underlying osmosis through direct observation of plasmolysis in onion epidermal cells.
      • The structure and properties of DNA through a simple extraction experiment using everyday materials.
      • The existence of synthetic biology as an emerging scientific field and the opportunities offered by student-led research initiatives such as iGEM.

      Most importantly, students experienced scientific concepts through observation and experimentation, allowing them to connect theoretical knowledge with tangible biological phenomena.

      What We Learned. This activity was equally valuable for our team, as it allowed us to better understand how secondary school students engage with biology education.

      We observed that many students were highly interested in laboratory science but had very limited opportunities to interact with scientific equipment or perform experiments themselves. Concepts that often appear abstract in school textbooks became significantly easier for students to understand once they were able to observe them directly.

      We also noticed that students were particularly enthusiastic when handling laboratory equipment and participating actively in experiments. Their questions demonstrated a strong curiosity about how biological research is conducted in real laboratories and what scientific careers involve beyond the classroom.

      These observations reinforced the importance of experiential learning and highlighted the need for greater exposure to practical science education at the secondary school level.

      Insights: What Changed in Our Understanding. This activity helped us identify several important points:

      • High school students are eager to engage with science when given opportunities for active participation.
      • Practical demonstrations significantly improve understanding of biological concepts that are often difficult to grasp through theory alone.
      • Many students have limited familiarity with laboratory equipment and research practices despite their interest in biological sciences.
      • Hands-on experimentation encourages curiosity, discussion, and critical thinking.
      • Students are highly motivated by activities that allow them to make connections between classroom knowledge and real-world scientific applications.
      • Introducing students to research communities such as iGEM helps make scientific careers and university-level research feel more accessible.

      The experience reinforced our belief that science education becomes more meaningful when students can directly observe and investigate the phenomena they study.

      This visit influenced the way we approach educational outreach as an iGEM team in several ways.

      First, it confirmed the value of incorporating practical activities whenever possible. We observed that students were most engaged when they could actively participate in experiments rather than simply listen to explanations. As a result, we became even more committed to designing interactive educational activities that emphasize hands-on learning.

      Second, the activity demonstrated that even relatively simple experiments can have a significant educational impact when they are connected to concepts students already encounter in school. This encouraged us to prioritize accessible and reproducible demonstrations that can be implemented in schools with limited laboratory infrastructure.

      Third, students' enthusiasm for learning about synthetic biology and university research highlighted the importance of introducing scientific opportunities at an earlier educational stage. We therefore became more intentional about incorporating discussions of research, innovation, and scientific careers into our outreach efforts.

      Scalability and Reproducibility. This activity can be easily reproduced by other iGEM teams, university student groups, or educators interested in promoting biological sciences. The basic requirements include:

      The activity can be adapted according to available resources and the educational background of the participants. Individual experiments can be conducted independently or combined into a larger workshop format. Because many of the materials are inexpensive and widely available, the activity is particularly suitable for schools with limited laboratory facilities.

      Furthermore, the structure can be modified to accommodate different age groups, making it a flexible and scalable model for promoting practical science education and scientific literacy.

      Interactive and engaging activities such as this one are crucial for encouraging young students to adopt a scientific mindset of curiosity and experimentation when the rigidity of the curriculum, the stress, and the pressure of exams may prove stifling. Through demonstrations and experiments like these, the students realize how their hard earned knowledge can be utilized for understanding real, observable biological processes. They realize that science can be fun, interesting, and engaging, all of which facilitate deep, lifelong learning. Ultimately, aside from fostering a positive learning environment, such activities are necessary in order to make science more accessible to younger generations, to foster love for research, and enhance science communication from academia to society.

      5th Evening EPAL of Thessaloniki

      The audience for this educational initiative consisted of students attending the 5th Evening Vocational High School (EPAL) of Thessaloniki. Unlike standard high school demographics, evening EPAL students represent a highly diverse group of adult learners and working individuals who balance daytime professional or family responsibilities with night school education. While these students chose a technical or specialised educational track, many lacked extensive exposure to the practical side of molecular biology. Our primary goal was to demystify complex genetic concepts, introduce them to the cutting-edge landscape of synthetic biology through our iGEM project, and break down the barriers between academic university research and the wider community.

      This educational visit took place in person at the school's facilities. Designed to accommodate the limited evening schedule of the students, the workshop was structured into three sequential, highly engaging segments over two school hours:

      • iGEM & project presentation. We initiated the session by introducing iGEM Thessaloniki, explaining the nature of the international competition, and pitching our team's current research project. We focused on how synthetic biology solves tangible, real-world problems.
      • Demystifying the Central Dogma. Moving from our project to fundamental science, we delivered an interactive presentation on the Central Dogma of Molecular Biology. We mapped out the flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA and proteins, using simple analogies to ensure the concept was accessible to students of all educational backgrounds.
      • Hands-on DNA extraction. To solidify the theoretical talk, we transitioned into a practical experiment. Using accessible household items (including dish soap, table salt and ethanol), we guided the students through a crude DNA extraction protocol. This gave them a frontline look at isolating the actual blueprint of life.

      What participants learned. Through active participation in our workshop, the students gained insights into:

      • The foundational pathway of genetic replication, transcription and translation via the Central Dogma.
      • The real-world applications of synthetic biology and how student-led initiatives like iGEM bridge the gap between innovation and society.
      • The biochemical principles behind cell lysis and nucleic acid precipitation through a tangible, visible experiment.
      • The understanding that scientific exploration does not require high-tech, multi-million-dollar setups, but can be conceptualised using everyday household components.

      What we learned. Engaging with an adult, working student body provided our team with an invaluable perspective on public science communication:

      • We recognised that evening school students possess an incredible work ethic and immense maturity, showing deep curiosity despite their demanding day-and-night schedules.
      • We realised that high-level molecular biology concepts can easily resonate with non-traditional students if stripped of rigid, textbook jargon and linked directly to practical demonstrations.

Alongside her academic journey, Evi is actively involved in science communication, combining her interest in physics with a passion for making scientific knowledge more accessible, engaging, and understandable to wider audiences.

                Our discussion followed the journey from being a school student with an interest in science, to entering university, discovering new academic interests, and gradually beginning to see yourself as a future researcher. Evi shared her perspective on how this transition does not happen all at once. University introduces students to new ways of thinking, greater independence, and a much wider range of possibilities than they may have been exposed to before.

                An important part of the conversation focused on the idea that students do not need to have their entire academic or professional future figured out from the beginning. Interests can change, new opportunities can appear, and experiences inside and outside the classroom can completely reshape the direction someone wants to follow. University courses, research groups, conversations with professors, extracurricular activities, and interactions with other students can all help someone understand what excites them most.

                We also discussed what it means to move from simply learning science to actively participating in it. Research requires students to become comfortable with asking questions that may not yet have clear answers. Curiosity, critical thinking, persistence, and the willingness to explore unfamiliar ideas gradually become just as important as the scientific knowledge gained through lectures and textbooks. In this sense, becoming a researcher is not a single milestone, but a continuous process of learning and discovery.

                A central theme of the episode was science communication. Evi highlighted that scientific knowledge should not remain confined within laboratories, universities, or academic publications. Researchers also need to be able to explain why their work matters and communicate complex ideas in ways that different audiences can understand. Effective science communication requires more than simply making terminology easier; it requires understanding the audience, choosing the right way to present information, and creating a meaningful connection between science and everyday life.

                This connection between science and society is especially important for younger students. When scientific topics are presented in an approachable and engaging way, science can feel less distant and intimidating. Science communication can therefore encourage curiosity, improve scientific literacy, challenge misconceptions, and help young people realize that scientific research is something they can also become part of.

                Throughout the discussion, one message remained particularly important: there is no single correct path into STEM or research. Every scientific journey develops differently, and students should give themselves the freedom to explore, change direction, ask questions, and pursue opportunities that genuinely interest them. Communication skills, curiosity, adaptability, and openness to new experiences can be just as valuable as technical knowledge.

                Through Evi's experience, the episode presented science as much more than a university subject or future profession. It is a process of constantly asking questions, learning from others, sharing knowledge, and finding new ways to connect scientific discovery with the world around us.

                Cancer Research Behind the Scenes : Eleni Zairi

                The episode welcomed Eleni Zairi, an oncologist whose academic journey began with studies at the Medical School of the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (AUTH). She went on to specialise in medical oncology and gained valuable international experience at the Gustave Roussy oncology centre in Paris, specialising in lung cancer. At the time of the discussion, she was serving as Assistant Director of the 4th Oncology Department at the Interbalkan Medical Center in Thessaloniki, where she leads a clinical trials department dedicated to holistic and optimal patient care.

                A central topic was the perception and realities of clinical trials. Zairi addressed the common taboos and fears surrounding oncological terms such as "cancer" or "neoplasm", noting that patients often worry about becoming "guinea pigs". However, she emphasised that through thorough patient education, these fears are largely alleviated. Modern oncology has expanded far beyond standard chemotherapy to include immunotherapies, targeted therapies, antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), which work by leveraging the patient's own immune system to fight cancer.

                The discussion explored the multi-phase journey of a cancer drug from the laboratory to the patient. In Phase 1 trials, research focuses heavily on the safety and optimal administration of a novel drug, closely monitoring a small cohort of global patients for unexpected side effects. Zairi highlighted that unexpected clinical reactions — such as a fever triggered by a receptor-specific drug — can actually indicate that the treatment is successfully working. International collaboration plays a vital role here, as researchers from Europe, America and Asia connect weekly to share data and refine trial protocols.

                Another major theme was the transition to later trial stages and efficiency. Phase 2 trials evaluate dosing variations and administration methods, such as replacing intravenous therapies with subcutaneous injections to reduce hospital stay durations and improve long-term patient autonomy. Phase 3 trials directly compare these innovative treatments against existing global guidelines to establish superior alternatives. Zairi noted that while drug approvals historically took two to three years, exceptional efficacy can now lead to FDA fast-track approvals, sometimes bringing breakthrough treatments to the frontier of oncology in as little as 8 to 10 months.

                Finally, the guest emphasised the power of communication, patient education and hope. By using simple language to explain complex biomedical mechanisms, healthcare teams help vulnerable patients navigate their treatment journey with less fear. The rapid evolution of clinical trials not only provides oncologists with a stronger arsenal of therapeutic choices but ultimately offers young scientists and patients a hopeful glimpse into the future of cancer care.
